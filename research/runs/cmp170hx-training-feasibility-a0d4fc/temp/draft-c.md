# Which Premise Is True: A Decision Procedure for the CMP 170HX Training Box

The documentation for this box is not wrong. It is under-specified in exactly one place, and that place is load-bearing for every number in it. The repo states "PCIe Gen2 x4" as a single fact. It is not a single fact. It is a compound of two independently fixable axes — generation, which the software unlocker changes, and width, which a 24-capacitor rework changes — and the four possible states differ by 8× in one-way bandwidth. Because the repo never says which state it measured, both the optimistic and the pessimistic readings of its own numbers are defensible from its own text. That is why the argument about this box has been unresolvable: not because one side is wrong, but because the premise that would decide it was never written down.

The same is true of three other quantities. The repo's all-reduce figure has no column name attached, and the two plausible columns differ by 1.6×. Its compute figure is a burst GEMM peak, not a sustained training rate, and the two differ by roughly 2×. Its sparse-attention verdict rests on a kernel family whose payoff sign flips with gather granularity and with the number of query heads per KV group — a config value nobody has looked up.

Four measurements settle all of it. They cost one command, one hour, one command, and one grep respectively. None of them has been run. This report's claim is that the *recommendation* — DDP, frozen Engram tables, ≤1B trainable, 32k context, no CSA2 — survives every branch of that measurement tree, while the *reasoning* offered for it does not survive any of them. That asymmetry is the useful result. A conclusion that is right for four mutually exclusive reasons is a conclusion you can act on; a conclusion that is right for one reason you have not checked is a guess.

Throughout, numbers carry an evidence tier. **[T1]** is measured on this box and is user-reported only — the corpus contains no on-box training, all-reduce, NCCL or DDP measurement at all, and both [[performancemd]] and [[club-170hxdocsbenchmarksmd-at-main-pixelmlclub-170hx-github]] say so explicitly about their own pages. **[T2]** is measured on adjacent or identical silicon by a third party with a receipt. **[T3]** is a number published in a paper. **[T4]** is arithmetic from the above, and is only as good as its inputs.

## Hardware Reality

Start with the compute ladder, because the apparent disagreement about this card is not a disagreement. It is six measurements on three axes, read as if they were six measurements of one quantity.

A stock card reports 6.41 TFLOPS [T2, [[performancemd]]]. Unlocked, the same page measures 164.4–192.7 TFLOPS bf16 across seven tools and a dozen cards, against a 202.1 TFLOPS arithmetic ceiling — "2048 × 70 SM × 1410 MHz, verified exact" [T2/T4]. A 10-minute `gpu-burn -tc` burn at an enforced 180 W cap produces 74.6–78.5 TFLOP/s per card on all four cards of a health-gate rig, with per-card clocks, temperatures and matmul correctness recorded [T2, [[readmemd-2]]]. The PWRBRK# A/B on a healthy unlocked card at 250 W measures 155.7 TFLOPS, and 39.3 when the power-brake line is asserted [T2, [[lessonsmd]]].

Read as a ladder rather than a dispute, these are coherent. The 6.41 band is the locked card. The 164–193 band is a burst GEMM peak at stock clocks on an unlocked card. The 74.6–78.5 band is a sustained ten-minute burn at a 180 W cap — the closest thing in the corpus to a training-shaped load. The 155.7 is a healthy card at 250 W. Nothing here contradicts anything else; the axes are lock state, power cap, and duration.

The defensible training denominator is therefore a range, not a point: **75–100 TFLOPS per card sustained at 250 W**, centre around 90, aggregate 375–500 TFLOPS [T4]. The upper end is unmeasured, and that is the honest statement. Three independent lines converge on it. The 180 W burn gives 74.6–78.5; scaling to 250 W at the sub-linear rate you expect when power goes as clock cubed gives 83–88. PaLM's Table 3 brackets it from both sides: Megatron–Turing NLG on A100 achieves 30.2% MFU, which is 61 TFLOPS per card against a 202.1 ceiling, and PaLM on TPU v4 achieves 46.2%, which is 93 TFLOPS per card [T3, [[palm-scaling-language-modeling-with-pathways]]]. A 90-TFLOP centre is 44.5% of this card's ceiling, which sits between those two anchors, where it should.

The repo's 810 TFLOPS aggregate — 5 × 162 — overstates sustained training throughput by 1.6–1.8× [T4]. That single error propagates into every overhead and wall-clock figure in the documentation, and it is the first thing measurement M2 would fix.

Now the link, which is where the under-specification lives.

[[physical-modsmd]] carries an explicit warning box: "The capacitor mod changes link width only. It never changes PCIe generation. A cap-modded card with no unlocker installed reports x16 at PCIe 1.0. Conversely, the unlocker changes generation only." The lspci signature of a stock card is unambiguous — `LnkCap: Speed 2.5GT/s, Width x16` against `LnkSta: Speed 2.5GT/s, Width x4 (downgraded)` — and the driver option `OPT_PCIE_LANE_DISABLE = 0x00000000` proves the lanes are not fused off, only negotiated down [T2, [[pcie-subsystemmd]]]. Gen2 was reached in software on 2026-07-24; Gen3 and Gen4 have never been reached on this silicon.

So there are four states, and they are not close together:

| State | How reached | One-way rate | Host-staged busbw | algbw at N=5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gen1 x4 | stock, no unlocker | 0.8 GB/s [T2] | ~0.45–0.55 GB/s [T4] | ~0.28–0.34 GB/s [T4] |
| Gen2 x4 | unlocker only | 1.68 GB/s [T2] | 0.94–1.07 GB/s [T4] | 0.58–0.67 GB/s [T4] |
| Gen1 x16 | capacitor mod only | 3.2 GB/s [T4] | ~1.8–2.1 GB/s [T4] | ~1.1–1.3 GB/s [T4] |
| Gen2 x16 | unlocker + capacitor mod | 6.63 GB/s [T2] | 3.69–4.24 GB/s [T2] | 2.3–2.65 GB/s [T4] |

The one-way figures are anchored. A hands-on review of four of these cards states plainly: "the transfer rate is 800MB/s at Gen 1 and 1.6GB/s at Gen 2" [T2, [[i-tested-the-cmp170hx]]]. The x16 figure comes from a sanitized NCCL receipt on three cards at Gen2 x16 behind PLX switches: 6.63–6.68 GB/s host-staged copy [T2, [[readmemd]]]. And the linearity of width is confirmed by a card that retrained at x2 and "ran exactly half speed everywhere (0.84 GB/s same-switch)" [T2, [[p2pmd]]].

The modal state is Gen2 x4. A four-card rig reports "All four slots negotiate Gen2 x4 (confirmed across cold power cycles; no P2P, `nvidia-smi topo -p2p` reports GNS everywhere)" [T2, [[readme_enmd]]]. That is the state the user's box is almost certainly in, because the capacitor mod is 24 × 0402 220nF X7R rework and nobody does that by accident. But "almost certainly" is not "measured", and the difference between Gen2 x4 and Gen2 x16 is 4× on the wire. Measurement M1 — `lspci -vv` on all five cards, reading `LnkCap` against `LnkSta` — is one command and it closes this permanently.

One more hardware fact matters and it is counter-intuitive. Peer-to-peer is absent by default, and the patch that enables it does not help. On a four-card PLX rig, static-BAR1 P2P works on all 12 ordered pairs at 5.79 GB/s each, every byte verified [T2, [[2026-10-01-cmp170hx-4card-bar1-p2p-plx-cudaipynb]]]. But the host-staged path on the same rig moves large copies at 6.27 GB/s — P2P is **8% slower** for anything above 16 MiB — and GLM-5.3-Flash TP4 decode drops from 396 to 293 tok/s with P2P on, a 26% regression. P2P's only real win is latency: 16 µs against 22 µs at 4 KiB. So P2P is a latency lever, not a bandwidth lever, and at training batch sizes it is a pessimization. The repo's decision to skip P2P work is correct, but for a better reason than it gives: even a working P2P patch would not move the number that matters.

## Training Topologies

The four topologies differ in what their communication volume scales with, and that difference — not the fabric — is what decides which one you can afford.

DDP and FSDP move O(parameters): the gradient payload is fixed by model size and independent of sequence length. PP moves O(tokens × hidden × stages): the activation hand-off is fixed by batch and width and independent of parameter count. TP moves O(tokens × hidden × layers): two all-reduces per layer on the critical path. EP moves O(tokens × top-k × hidden × (EP−1)/EP) per all-to-all.

The repo's ranking — DDP < FSDP < PP < TP < EP by bytes on the wire — is correct. Its magnitudes are wrong in both directions, and the errors happen to cancel into a conclusion that is right for the frozen-table design and wrong for the axis the goal document actually states. One correction belongs up front: FSDP's multiplier over DDP is **1.5×**, not 2×. FSDP moves 3Ψ against DDP's 2Ψ, stated identically by the FSDP paper and ZeRO [T3, [[zero-memory-optimizations-toward-training-trillion-parameter-models-sc20]]], and the 2× figure only reproduces if you force fp32 gradient reduction rather than accepting FSDP's default low-precision reduce [T3, [[zero-3-vs-fsdp-memory-efficiency-for-llm-training-lyceum]]]. The repo's table also compares FSDP at 7B trainable against DDP at 1B trainable, which confounds the parameter counts and inflates the ratio.

Take PP first, because it is the topology the repo dismissed and should not have. At 1M tokens and 32k context with a 7B-trainable model at hidden 2048, PP=5 puts 22.4 GB of weights plus optimizer state on each stage, plus roughly 3.4 GB of in-flight activations, for **~25.8 GB per card** [T4]. That fits 64 GB with 38 GB of headroom, and it trains the 7B experts without freezing anything. DDP at 7B trainable needs 112 GB per card and does not fit at all [T4]. The traffic is 32.8 GB per step including backward hand-offs, but it is not additive: in 1F1B each stage sends one hand-off while computing the previous micro-batch. Per-hop hand-off at 32 micro-batches of 32k tokens is 32k × 2048 × 2 B = **134 MB**, which at 1.68 GB/s takes 80 ms, against a per-micro-batch forward+backward compute window of 6 × 200M × 32k / 162 TFLOPS = **243 ms** [T4]. The hand-off hides. What remains is the bubble: (5−1)/32 = 12.5% for 1F1B, 11.1% by GPipe's formula, ~4.2% for zero-bubble H1, and under 4.5% measured for RoundPipe with asynchronous optimizer updates [T3/T2]. So PP's overhead is **4–14%**, not the ~290% the repo's table implies. The club's own measurement agrees that PP is link-tolerant: MiMo PP3 decodes at 74.6 tok/s at Gen2 x16 against 75.5 tok/s at Gen1 x16 — a 1.2% difference across a 4× change in link rate, because pipeline-parallel decode is HBM-bound rather than link-bound [T2, [[club-170hxdocscmp170hx-understandingmd-at-main-pixelmlclub-170hx-github]]].

The repo's PP row is wrong not because its volume is wrong — 16.4 GB is the forward half of a 32.8 GB total — but because it models PP's traffic as additive when 1F1B exists precisely to overlap it. [[seq1f1b-efficient-sequence-level-pipeline-parallelism-for]] makes the trade-off explicit: pipeline schedules trade bubble ratio against memory footprint, and at 32K-to-128K sequences the conventional schedules pay both. That is the real cost of PP, and it is a scheduling cost, not a bandwidth cost.

Now the correction that matters most. At 32k context, the quadratic attention term inflates compute by roughly 4.2× against a gradient payload that does not move at all. That is what rescues DDP. The repo computed its 36% overhead for a 1B-active model at 6 FLOPs per parameter per token and 810 TFLOPS: 6 × 10⁹ × 10⁶ = 6.0 × 10¹⁵ FLOPs, or 7.4 s, against a 2.7 s all-reduce. At 32k the same model needs 2.53 × 10¹⁶ FLOPs per 1M-token step, which at 450 TFLOPS is 56 s, against an all-reduce of 2.5–4.0 s depending on which column the 0.805 GB/s figure belongs to — **4.4% to 7.1%** [T4]. The 36% is not wrong. It is the overhead for the *4k baseline run*, and the repo applied it to its 32k arms. Both numbers are right; they belong to different experiments. That is the whole of the "denominator war" in one sentence.

Which means the fabric does not bind the DDP arm at 32k in any of the four link states. At Gen1 x4, with busbw around 0.5 GB/s, the 3.2 GB per-rank wire volume takes 6.4 s against 56 s — 11%. At Gen2 x16, with busbw 3.7 GB/s, it takes 0.86 s — 1.5%. The range across the entire measurement tree is **1.5–11.4%**, centre 4–7%.

That has a consequence the repo did not draw. PP's advantage over DDP is 4–14% bubble against 1–12% all-reduce. At 32k, those are the same number. PP is not worth the systems work — the stage balancing, the static-shape constraint, the custom scheduler — to buy a difference inside the noise of your own unmeasured premise. PP becomes the right answer only if you insist on training the 7B experts, where DDP's 112 GB/card does not fit and PP's 25.8 GB/card does.

Two community measurements calibrate this, and they disagree in a way that is instructive.

The first is a 13-hour case study on a workstation [T2, [[a-13-hour-training-experiment-on-a-pc-workstation-why-optimization-begins-with-m]]]. Four GPUs on PCIe 4 with no high-speed interconnect, a 500M-parameter MoE with ~330M active, ~100k tokens per microbatch, and `gradient_accumulation=10` for a 1M-token effective batch. Measured: "gradient communication accounted for 50% of the single-step time, while matrix multiplication took up only about 20%"; a single all-reduce took ~0.08 s; across ~101,000 microbatches that is 2.26 hours of synchronization. Enabling `no_sync()` on the first nine backward passes — so the all-reduce fires once per ten microbatches — took the run from 18 hours to 13.2 hours. This is the empirical shape the repo's model predicts, and it validates gradient accumulation as the lever.

The second is a 10-GPU RTX 3090 cluster over PCIe with no P2P, sharing ~3 GB/s [T2, [[pcie-110-gpu-스케일링]]]. DDP scaling: 1→2 GPUs is 1.14× (57% efficiency), 1→4 is 1.32× (33%), 1→8 is 2.16× (27%), 1→10 is 1.91× (19%). Read naively, that says a slow fabric destroys DDP. But their test model is 305M parameters at sequence length 512 with batch size 4 — a workload where compute per step is trivial and communication is everything. At 32k context with 1B active parameters, compute per step is roughly a thousand times larger against the same payload. The scaling-efficiency curve is a function of the compute-to-communication ratio, not of the fabric alone. That is why the same ~3 GB/s fabric gives 19% at 512 tokens and 4% at 32k.

The mechanism is cleaner than the arithmetic. DDP's all-reduce is overlapped with the backward pass, so it is free until the payload exceeds the backward window. On 8×A100 with 80 GB cards, DDP reaches 7.99× on 8 GPUs — near-ideal — because "the gradient all-reduce is fully overlapped with backward compute" [T2, [[perf-29-throughput-by-switching-from-deepspeed-to-ddp-issue-177-tensorautoopen-2]]]. At 32k the step's compute is 56 s and the all-reduce is 2.5–4.0 s, so it is entirely hidden. At 4k the compute is 7.4 s and the all-reduce is the same 2.5–4.0 s, so a third to a half of it is exposed. The 36%-to-7% shift is an overlap story, not a bandwidth story.

There is one free lever the repo has not pulled. Critical batch size for OLMo 1B and 7B plateaus around 4096 documents at 4096 tokens per document — **~16.8M tokens** [T3, [[critical-batch-size-revisited-a-simple-empirical]]]. At a 1M-token global batch you are 17× below critical batch size, which means you can raise the global batch to 4–8M tokens and cut all-reduce frequency by 4–8× at no cost in loss. On a bandwidth-starved fabric that is the cheapest available lever, and it is strictly better than buying P2P patches or capacitor rework. The repo's choice of 1M is safe but conservative; 4M is safe and four times cheaper on the wire.

## DDP Overhead Model

The repo's overhead model is arithmetically correct and its inputs are mislabelled. That is a more interesting failure than being wrong.

The all-reduce figure is 0.75 GiB/s [T1], described as "measured by the user", with no command, no message size, no repetition count, and — critically — no column name. nccl-tests reports two bandwidth columns, and they differ by a fixed factor. `AllReduceGetBw` computes `baseBw = count × typesize / 1e9 / sec` and then `busBw = baseBw × 2(n−1)/n` [T2, [[2026-09-02-cmp170hx-health-gateipynb]]]. At N=5 that factor is exactly 1.6. So busbw = 1.6 × algbw by construction, and busbw is *defined* as the per-rank link rate — the number that "should reflect the speed of the hardware bottleneck."

Three readings of 0.75 GiB/s = 0.805 GB/s are therefore possible, and they point in opposite directions:

| Reading | Implied algbw | Implied busbw | Verdict against physics |
|---|---|---|---|
| algbw | 0.805 GB/s | 1.29 GB/s | busbw would be 77% of the 1.68 GB/s one-way rate on a fabric where every byte crosses PCIe twice — implausible |
| busbw | 0.503 GB/s | 0.805 GB/s | 75–96% of the calibrated host-staged band — plausible |
| aggregate | 0.161 GB/s | 0.258 GB/s | 4–6× below the community's own ~1.0 GB/s at the same width — implausible |

The calibrated band comes from the club's own x16 receipt: NCCL achieves 3.69–4.24 GB/s busbw against a 6.63–6.68 GB/s one-way rate, which is 55.7–64.0% of one-way [T2/T4]. Scaling by the measured width ratio 1.68/6.63 = 0.253 gives x4 busbw of **0.94–1.07 GB/s** and algbw of **0.58–0.67 GB/s**. The community's own Gen2 x4 figure — "~1.0 GB/s measured bus bandwidth" [T2, [[lessonsmd]]] — lands inside that band. Two independent routes agree.

So the repo's 0.805 GB/s is 75–86% of the plausible busbw band, which makes it a slightly conservative measurement, and 1.29–1.48× the plausible algbw band, which makes it not a measurement of that column at all. If it is busbw, the repo's arithmetic is wrong by 1.6× because it divides a payload by a busbw and calls the result a payload time. If it is algbw, the number is physically unreachable on a double-traversal fabric. Either way the model needs a correction, and measurement M3 — `all_reduce_perf` at 256 MiB with both columns labelled, N=5 — settles which.

Here is the decomposition that matters, holding the payload at 2.0 GB of bf16 gradients and the model at 1B trainable parameters. Note the wire arithmetic: a ring all-reduce sends `2(N−1)/N × S` bytes per rank, which at N=5 is 1.6 × 2.0 GB = **3.2 GB on the wire per GPU**, and the time is that wire volume divided by busbw.

| Configuration | Compute per 1M-token step | All-reduce time | Overhead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repo: 6ND, 810 TFLOPS, 0.805 GB/s treated as algbw | 7.4 s | 2.5 s | **34%** (repo says 36%) |
| Sustained denominator only: 6ND, 450 TFLOPS | 13.3 s | 2.5 s | **19%** |
| 32k context, 450 TFLOPS, 0.805 GB/s treated as algbw | 56.2 s | 2.5 s | **4.4%** |
| 32k, 450 TFLOPS, busbw reading (3.2 GB wire at 0.805 GB/s) | 56.2 s | 4.0 s | **7.1%** |
| 32k, 450 TFLOPS, Gen1 x4 busbw (~0.5 GB/s) | 56.2 s | 6.4 s | **11.4%** |
| 32k, 450 TFLOPS, Gen2 x16 busbw (3.7 GB/s) | 56.2 s | 0.86 s | **1.5%** |

The 36% is reproducible from the repo's own inputs, which is why it looked solid. It is the overhead of a short-context run at burst peak. The 32k arms sit at 1.5–11.4%. The repo's headline — "DDP's overhead depends only on batch size, not model size" — is true for dense models and false for MoE, and it is true at fixed context and false across context lengths. Both of those qualifications matter, and neither is in the document.

The same denominator error destroys the throughput prediction, and here the failure is not a matter of degree.

At 100% MFU of the measured 202.1 TFLOPS ceiling, five cards deliver 1010.5 TFLOPS. A 500M model at 32k needs 2.554 × 10¹⁰ FLOPs per token, so the **absolute maximum is 39.6k tokens per second** [T4]. The repo predicts 80–120k tok/s at 32k. That requires 2.0–3.0 PFLOPS aggregate, or 400–600 TFLOPS per card, which is 2–3× the hardware ceiling. The prediction is not optimistic. It is physically impossible, before any MFU discount.

With a realistic denominator the picture is worse. At 30.2% MFU the box does 305 TFLOPS and 11.9k tok/s; at 46.2% it does 467 TFLOPS and 18.3k tok/s; at FlashAttention-2's best-case 72% it does 727 TFLOPS and 28.5k tok/s [T3/T4, [[flashattention-2]]]. Adding PaLM's +25% rematerialization cost — the repo plans full activation checkpointing, which is closer to +33% — and the 4–7% DDP overhead gives **~9–22k tok/s, centre ~15k**. The 9B-token run therefore takes **~5–12 days, centre ~7 days, not 25 hours** [T4].

This is the single largest error in the documentation, and it is not a hardware disagreement. It is a FLOPs-model disagreement. PaLM's Appendix B gives the canonical formula as `6N + 12·L·H·Q·T` per token, where `H·Q` is the attention inner width [T3, [[palm-scaling-language-modeling-with-pathways]]]. The same decomposition appears independently in the measurement literature as `f_token = 6P + 12·n_l·h·s`, with the explicit caveat that MFU computed this way is "a modeled fraction of vendor peak, not a hardware-counter measurement" [T3, [[readmemd-faircslmtrainbench-at-main]]]. The repo's 6ND is the non-attention half of that formula with the attention half dropped. For the repo's own 494–500M, 28-layer, 16-head × head-dim-128 model at 32k, attention is **88% of the step** and total FLOPs are **8.5×** the 6ND estimate [T4]. At 4k the same model is 48% attention and 1.94× 6ND; at 1k it is 19% and 1.24×. So 6ND is a perfectly good convention for large models at short context — PaLM 540B at 2k has attention at 1.6% of FLOPs — and it is simply the wrong convention for a 500M model at 32k. Megatron-Core's measured statement corroborates the shape: "at 64K tokens, SDPA consumes 69% of FLOPs, compared to only 10–15% in short-sequence scenarios" [T3, [[scalable-training-of-mixture-of-experts-models-with]]].

The repo's 32k haircut was 1.2–1.75× (140k → 80–120k). The true FLOPs ratio from 4k to 32k is **4.39×** [T4]. That single mismatch is the whole error.

## MoE & EP

The repo rules out expert parallelism with a ~72× cost estimate. The estimate is about 12× too high, and the conclusion is nonetheless correct — for a reason the repo never states.

The arithmetic error is a familiar one: aggregate volume divided by per-GPU rate. The dispatch-plus-combine traffic across 24 layers at 1M tokens is roughly 400 GB aggregate, but each GPU sends only its share. Megatron-Core's formula for per-GPU send volume in each all-to-all is `T·K·h·(EP−1)/EP` [T3, [[scalable-training-of-mixture-of-experts-models-with]]]. At 1M tokens, top-k 2, hidden 2048, EP=5, that is (10⁶/5) × 2 × 2048 × 4 × 2 B = **6.55 GB per layer per GPU** for dispatch, or 13.1 GB with the combine, and **314 GB per GPU per step** across 24 layers — 390 s at 0.805 GB/s against 56 s of compute at 32k, or **~7×** [T4]. Not 72×. The same aggregate-versus-per-GPU confusion inflates the repo's PP row, which is why both numbers looked catastrophic.

That correction has a consequence the repo did not anticipate. Tensor parallelism at the same configuration moves 2 all-reduces × 24 layers × 10⁶ tokens × 2048 hidden × 2 B = 196.6 GB of tensor volume, which on a 5-way ring is 1.6× that on the wire, or **314.6 GB per GPU per step** — 391 s, or **~7×** [T4]. So at 32k, EP and TP cost the same. The repo's claim that TP is "the same shape as EP" is accidentally right at this configuration, though for the wrong reason: both scale with tokens × hidden × layers, and at top-k 2 the constants coincide. At 4k both are ~29×. The repo's 72× is an order of magnitude too high, and the ~28× figure that a short-context denominator produces is not the number that applies to a 32k run.

The qualitative difference the arithmetic misses is placement. TP's two all-reduces per layer sit on the critical path, serialized with the layer's compute. EP's all-to-all can overlap with expert computation, which is why communication-computation overlap is the standard EP optimization. So equal volume does not mean equal cost: TP's is exposed, EP's is hideable. That is the real reason TP is worse, and it is a scheduling property rather than a bandwidth one.

But ~7× is still prohibitive, and the stronger objection to EP is not arithmetic at all. It is that no EP library runs on this fabric. [[efficient-expert-parallel-communication]] states the barrier as a closed list: "Communication libraries specialized for MoE EP, including DeepEP, pplx-kernels, NIXL-EP, NCCL EP, and UCCL-EP, similarly rely on NVLink, GPUDirect P2P, or RDMA for their optimized GPU-initiated paths," and "existing EP communication libraries cannot operate on these systems." Without P2P, "every inter-GPU transfer must instead be staged through a bounce buffer in CPU memory," and NCCL's ring all-gather then moves `2(N−1)S` bytes per GPU against `N·S` for a single-step host broadcast — a 1.6× penalty at N=5.

The paper that documents this barrier also proves the barrier is not absolute. ThunderEP achieves 2.00× dispatch and 1.53× combine over NCCL on PCIe-connected RTX 4090 and 5090 systems, and up to 1.66× end-to-end over state-of-the-art MoE inference frameworks [T2]. Host-staged EP is possible. But ThunderEP is integrated into vLLM and evaluated on inference; it has no gradient collectives. So the honest statement is not "EP is impossible" but "EP is possible for inference and unimplemented for training." That distinction matters because it tells you what to watch: the day someone ships a training-side ThunderEP, EP becomes a live option on this box.

The regime dependence matters for how TP is described. At decode (T=1) TP is latency-bound — 86 collectives per forward, each a host-staged round trip — which is what the club measured as a flat ~800 tok/s prefill curve and 60.4 tok/s decode [T2, [[club-170hxdocstopology-and-parallelismmd-at-main-pixelmlclub-170hx-github-2]]]. At training batch sizes the tensors are 134 MB and TP is bandwidth-bound. The repo applied a decode-era intuition to a training question, and then got the magnitude wrong in the other direction.

The asymmetry that actually decides the MoE question is one the repo half-states. DDP's reducer buckets the *full* parameter set regardless of which experts saw tokens; an expert that received zero tokens contributes a zero gradient but still occupies its slice of the all-reduce buffer. Megatron-Core states both halves in one paragraph: "All E experts' parameters, gradients, and optimizer states must reside in memory," and "Per-token FLOPs scale only with N_active (∝K), not N_total (∝E), leaving insufficient computation to overlap with the growing communication… This is not a matter of degree; it is a qualitative difference from dense models" [T3].

So the correct invariance is not "overhead depends only on batch size." It is `overhead ≈ (constant / T_global) × (N_total / N_active)`. At 32k and 450 TFLOPS the constant gives 4.4–7.1% for a dense model; for the goal document's 7B-total/1B-active axis it gives **31–50%** [T4]. That is not the 252% that the repo's 6ND-and-810-TFLOPS model implies once the total-to-active ratio is applied, but it is still the wrong topology for that axis. PP at 25.8 GB/card and a 4–14% bubble is the answer if you want to train the experts.

Which yields a clean split. Claim (a), "DDP is the only viable topology at 1M-token global batches," is **true for the frozen-table design** — where trainable equals active equals 1B, DDP's overhead is 4.4–7.1% at 32k, and no other topology beats it by more than the width of its own unmeasured premise. It is **false for the stated 7B/1B MoE axis**, where DDP does not fit on 64 GB cards at all. Claim (b), "expert and tensor parallelism are ruled out by the fabric," is **true**, but EP is ruled out by a software barrier rather than a 72× cost, and TP is ruled out because its ~7× of traffic sits on the critical path rather than being hideable, not because it is "the same shape as EP."

## Engram & Frozen Memory

The frozen-table argument is the one place where the repo's arithmetic is right, its citation is wrong, and its conclusion is right for reasons it does not give.

Start with the citation. [[memory-grafting-scaling-language-model]] says: "For each n-gram order, we select the top 1M most frequent n-grams and compute their frozen memory representations offline, yielding 3M entries per grafted layer. Each entry stores a hidden-state vector of the grafting model in bfloat16; for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, this corresponds to roughly 12 GB of frozen memory per layer." Three million entries × 2048 dimensions × 2 bytes = 12.29 GB per layer; two grafted layers (donor layers 8 and 24) = **24.58 GB, or 12.29B parameters** [T3/T4]. The Figure 4 caption confirms the scale: "0.92B trainable parameters with approximately 12B frozen grafting memory offloaded."

The repo's §3 says "33M entries per grafted layer (top 11M n-grams per order)" while keeping "roughly 12 GB per layer." Thirty-three million entries × 2048 × 2 B = 135.3 GB per layer. That is an **11× internal inconsistency** with its own 12 GB figure [T4]. Its §5 then budgets "6B frozen" = 12 GB total, which is exactly one layer of the paper's table. So the repo's frozen table is half the paper's configuration, and its per-layer entry count is off by an order of magnitude. Neither error is fatal to the conclusion, but both would be fatal to a reproduction attempt.

The memory arithmetic itself is sound for the repo's own design. A frozen parameter has no gradient accumulator and no optimizer state, so it costs exactly its bf16 rows: 7B × 2 B (weights) + 1B × 2 B (gradients) + 1B × 12 B (Adam's fp32 master, m, and v) = **28 GB**, plus 3.2 GB of checkpointed activations = 31.2 GB [T4]. That fits 64 GB with 33 GB of headroom.

Two corrections tighten it. First, the 3.2 GB activation estimate — "32,768 tokens × 2048 hidden × 2 B × 24 layers" — counts only checkpointed layer inputs and silently assumes a fused cross-entropy. At 32k × 151,669 vocabulary × 2 B, the LM head produces **9.94 GB** bf16 if materialized, or 19.88 GB fp32 [T4]. With fused linear cross-entropy that drops to ~0.6 GB, so the 3.2 GB figure is valid — but only under an assumption the document does not state. Without it the budget is 41.8 GB, which still fits but leaves 22 GB of headroom rather than 33.

Second, and more importantly, freezing is **not a memory necessity**. The repo's headline — "7B fully trainable with Adam needs ~112 GB per card — it does not fit DDP on 64 GB" — is correct for plain DDP plus Adam (7B × 16 B/param = 112 GB) and tests only that one configuration. Three alternatives fit:

| Route | Bytes per param | Model state | Total with activations | Fits 64 GB? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DDP + Adam (fp32 master + m + v) | 16 | 112 GB | 115.8 GB | No |
| DDP + 8-bit Adam (block-wise quantized) | 6 | 42 GB | 45.9 GB | Yes |
| DDP + Adafactor (bf16 w+g, factored state) | 4 | 28 GB | 31.9 GB | Yes |
| ZeRO-2 at 5 ranks + Adam | 4.8 | 33.6 GB | 37.5 GB | Yes |

The 8-bit route is validated to 1.5B parameters with block-wise quantization [T3, [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2022]]]. Adafactor's factored second moment gives sublinear memory and, with momentum added back, loss comparable to Adam across 150M–1.2B [T3, [[adafactor-adaptive-learning-rates-with-sublinear-memory-cost]]]. ZeRO-2 at 5 ranks gives 4.8 bytes per parameter with "exactly 2Ψ" communication — the same byte volume as DDP — and keeps Adam, so comparability is preserved [T3, [[zero-memory-optimizations-toward-training-trillion-parameter-models-sc20]]]. All three fit with 18–32 GB of headroom. So "does not fit" is true of one configuration, not of the problem.

Freezing is nonetheless the right choice on this box, for two reasons the repo does not state.

The first is that at this scale the trained-table baseline is *negative*. The paper's 0.92B/50B-token table reads: MoE 45.62, vanilla Engram **45.03**, MG-GLM 45.83, MG-DeepSeek 46.42, MG-Qwen3.5 46.98 [T3]. Vanilla Engram loses to MoE by 0.59 points at 0.92B, and the paper attributes it to "the limited Engram embedding table in 0.9B model, which lead to more hash collisions." At 2.8B the sign flips: MoE 51.95, vanilla Engram 52.43, MG 53.86. So the same architecture axis is −0.59 at 0.92B and +0.48 at 2.8B. On a ≤1B-active box, freezing plus grafting is the only configuration in the paper with a positive sign.

The second is fabric cost, and this is where the branch structure appears. Training the 6B table rather than freezing it means its gradients must move. Under DDP the gradient tensor is dense — 12 GB at bf16 — so the all-reduce moves 1.6 × 12 = 19.2 GB per rank. At 0.805 GB/s that is ~24 s against 56 s of compute at 32k, or **~42% step-time penalty**; under the Engram recipe of sharded tables plus all-to-all, the traffic is ~10 KB/token × 1M tokens forward plus the same backward = 20 GB per step, or **~44%** [T4]. Freezing removes both.

But that penalty is a function of the link, and the link is the unmeasured premise. At Gen2 x16 with 3.7 GB/s busbw, the 19.2 GB gradient all-reduce takes 5.2 s against 56 s — **9%**, and the all-to-all route 10%. At Gen1 x4 with ~0.5 GB/s busbw, it takes 38 s — **68%**, and the all-to-all route 71%. So freezing is *preferable* on a fast link and *necessary* on a slow one. That is exactly the boundary condition the report is looking for: **M1 and M3 together decide whether the frozen-table design is load-bearing or merely convenient.** The repo asserts the strong version without having measured the premise that makes it strong.

Two further caveats belong here. The repo's 86% frozen allocation is far outside both published allocation laws: Engram's U-shaped optimum puts "roughly 20%–25%" of the sparse parameter budget into memory rather than experts, stable across ρ ≈ 75–80% [T3, [[conditional-memory-via-scalable-lookup]]], and LongCat allocates "no more than 50% of the total parameter budget to N-gram Embedding" [T3]. And the quality critique is real: static lookup cannot resolve polysemy or filter hash collisions, which is why Engram adds a learned scalar gate, and why [[dr-robert-li-engram-a-critical-analysis-of-conditional-memory-as-a-sparsity-axis]] frames conditional memory as a sparsity axis that needs gating rather than a free capacity knob. Production memory models overwhelmingly *train* their tables — Engram-27B carries 5.7B trained Engram parameters, UltraMemV2 trains "120B sparse memory parameters from scratch jointly with the backbone" [T3, [[ultramemv2-memory-networks-scaling-to-120b]]]. Freezing is a niche, and the repo is choosing the niche deliberately without saying so.

## CSA2 at 32k

The repo's verdict on compressed sparse attention — "worth one experiment, most likely to disappoint" — is wrong in both directions. CSA2 is not runnable on this box at all, and the sparse-attention family that *is* runnable has a measured 6.3× forward and 3.4× backward win at 32k on A100.

The memory objection is soft, and this is measured rather than argued. [[streamindex-memory-bounded-compressed-sparse]] identifies the real gate: not the attention kernel, but the indexer's score tensor. Public CSA implementations materialize a `[B, S, H_I, T]` FP32 intermediate before the head-sum. At S=32,768 with V4-Flash dimensions that is a 64 GB intermediate before the head-sum and a separate 1 GB intermediate after, for a measured peak of **129.00 GB** and 317 ms on a 140 GB H200 — twice the capacity of a 64 GB CMP 170HX card [T2]. At S=65,536 it is 256 GB and OOMs outright.

The fix is a chunked partition-merge top-k driver that never materializes the full intermediate. On the same hardware it runs the same indexer at **0.40 GB and 10.3× faster** at 32k, and extends the runnable regime from 32K to 1,048,576 tokens with 6.21 GB peak — a 32× extension, with set-overlap recall bit-exact where both paths fit and mean recall rounding to 1.0000 across three design-space sweeps [T2]. So the 64–129 GiB figure is a 32× reducible implementation artifact, not a hardware wall. The repo's memory objection is answerable in software.

The architecture objection is not. Every training-capable CSA-class kernel in the corpus is Hopper-or-newer. StreamIndex's own Triton forward-and-backward stack is "Tested on H100 / H200 (Hopper, `sm_90a`)" and lists "multi-hardware validation on H100, A100, and MI300X" as future work [T2]. The Flash Sparse Attention paper benchmarks on H20 and H200 only [T3, [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2026]]]. TileLang's DSA kernels are built around `wgmma`, which is Hopper-only; Ampere has only `mma.sync`, and the one recorded fallback runs 1.81× slower. The only sm_80 path that exists is inference-only, with no backward. A GA100 box is two architecture generations below the floor of the entire production DSA stack.

So CSA2 is not an experiment on this box. It is a kernel-porting project: write an sm_80 indexer backward and an sm_80 sparse-MLA backward, tune both to usable MFU, then validate the distillation loss — before you learn anything about whether the architecture helps.

But the question underneath the CSA2 proposal has a positive answer, and the sign is decidable before writing a line of kernel code. It is set by gather granularity.

Token-granular selection loses. [[longcat-sparse-attention-taming-the-lightning-via]] measures LSA against dense MLA per attention layer at 32K: **1.08× forward, 0.79× backward, 0.83× total**, with crossover only at 64K (1.39×) [T2]. Since 0.83× is a speedup ratio, LSA takes 1/0.83 = **1.20× dense MLA's latency** — a net 20% loss. The mechanism is quantified: "each selected token is fetched via an independent gather of a single latent KV vector (1,152 B in BF16), which spans only 3 cachelines… this gather occupies merely 3 of the ~50 outstanding memory slots (yielding ~6% memory-level parallelism); … data packing efficiency is only ~75%. The resulting net effective bandwidth thus plummets to a mere ~4.5% (~1/22) of peak." That is why 64× fewer FLOPs produces a 20% slowdown.

Fine-block selection wins. [[native-sparse-attention-hardware-aligned-and-natively]] measures, on an 8-GPU A100 system with Triton kernels, **6.3× forward and 3.4× backward at 32k** (2.1×/1.1× at 8k, 3.8×/2.0× at 16k, 9.0×/6.0× at 64k) [T2]. Its config is fine-grained — compression block 32, selection block 64, 16 selected blocks, sliding window 512 — so 1,536 of 32,768 tokens are attended, a 4.69% density and 21.3× theoretical reduction. The measured 6.3× forward is about 30% of theoretical, which is what you expect from a memory-bound sparse attention kernel. And it improves quality: average benchmark 0.469 against 0.443 for full attention at 27B parameters and 260B tokens.

The independent Triton implementation corroborates the crossover. [[github-fla-orgnative-sparse-attention-efficient-triton-implementations-for-nativ]] publishes forward and backward timings against FlashAttention-2: at 4,096 tokens NSA is **0.72× forward and 0.61× backward** — slower than dense; at 8,192 it is 1.35×/1.06×; at 16,384 it is **2.66×/1.76×**. The crossover sits between 8k and 16k on that kernel, and 32k is comfortably past it. So the sign is not a property of "sparse attention." It is a property of block size against sequence length, and it flips inside a single implementation.

The fourth measurement lives here. Vanilla NSA's kernel forces a loop order that is efficient only when each GQA group contains a relatively large number of query heads. [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2026]] states the constraint and the consequence: existing LLMs "widely adopt a much smaller number of query heads in each GQA group," and "when the sequence length is 32K, one GQA group contains one query head, NSA consistently falls short of full attention, while FSA demonstrates superior performance than full attention." FSA delivers up to 3.5× kernel-level latency reduction and 1.6× on average against vanilla NSA, up to 1.25× and 1.09× end-to-end training speedup against NSA, and up to 2.47× and 1.86× against full attention [T3].

So measurement M4 is a one-line config check: count query heads per KV group in the planned model. The repo's 24-layer, 2048-hidden design with 16 heads has g = 16 if KV heads = 1 (multi-query), g = 4 if KV heads = 4 (grouped), and g = 1 if KV heads = 16 (multi-head). At g = 4 the vanilla NSA kernel is in its inefficient regime and FSA is the right implementation. At g = 1 and 32k, vanilla NSA loses to dense attention outright. This is knowable in a grep, and it decides whether the kernel you would reach for is the one that wins.

One caveat on the quality claim. NSA pretrained 270B tokens at 8k, then continued training and supervised fine-tuning on 32k texts with YaRN [T3]. The repo plans from-scratch 32k training from step one — a regime the published long-context literature reaches by laddering, not by starting there. [[skyladder-better-and-faster-pretraining-via]] is the canonical statement of the alternative: begin at a small context and expand along a linear schedule to the target window, and it reports that this beats naive long-context training at a fixed token budget for 1B models up to 32K and 3B models up to 8K, on 100B tokens [T3]. The *kernel* speedup is a property of the kernel and transfers; the *quality* result — 0.469 against 0.443 — is an adaptation-stage result and does not. That distinction does not invalidate the efficiency case, but it means the repo cannot cite the quality number as evidence that a from-scratch 32k sparse run will match dense.

## Verdict

Four claims, scored.

**(a) DDP is the only viable topology at 1M-token global batches.** True for the frozen-table design, false for the stated 7B/1B MoE axis. At 32k with ≤1B trainable, DDP's overhead is 4.4–7.1% and no alternative beats it by more than the uncertainty in the premise. At 7B trainable, DDP needs 112 GB per card and does not fit; PP fits at 25.8 GB per card with a 4–14% bubble. The claim is a statement about a design, not about the box, and the document conflates the two.

**(b) Expert and tensor parallelism are ruled out by the fabric.** True, but for different reasons than given. EP is blocked by a software barrier — no training-capable EP library runs without NVLink, P2P, or RDMA — and its cost is ~7×, not ~72×. TP is bandwidth-bound at training batch sizes and costs ~7× in volume but on the critical path, so it is not "the same shape as EP" in cost. The conclusion survives; the numbers do not.

**(c) Frozen Engram tables make a 7B-total / ~1B-trainable model fit DDP on 64 GB cards.** True as arithmetic (31.2 GB with fused cross-entropy, 41.8 GB without), but freezing is not a memory necessity — ZeRO-2, Adafactor, and 8-bit Adam all fit a fully-trainable 7B on 64 GB. Freezing is correct on this box because the trained-table baseline is negative at ≤1B and because training the table costs 9–71% of step time depending on the link. The claim is right; the justification is inverted.

**(d) CSA2 compressed sparse attention pays off at 32k training context.** False as stated. CSA2 is not runnable on sm_80 — every training-capable kernel in the stack is Hopper-or-newer, and the only sm_80 path is inference-only with no backward. The underlying question has a positive answer via fine-block sparse attention (6.3× forward, 3.4× backward at 32k on A100), and the sign is decidable before any kernel work by checking gather granularity and GQA group size.

Now the decision procedure. Four measurements, none of which the documentation ran:

| # | Measurement | Cost | What it decides | Decision rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | `lspci -vv` on all five cards; read `LnkCap` vs `LnkSta` for speed, width, and the "downgraded" flag | one command | Which of four link states is real | Gen2 x16 → fabric is not binding anywhere; Gen1 x4 → fabric binds the frozen-table question |
| M2 | Sustained bf16 matmul burn at the actual power cap, 10+ minutes, with clock telemetry | one hour | 75–100 vs 162–193 TFLOPS/card | Below 100 → wall-clock is the binding constraint; above 150 → the repo's schedule is closer to feasible |
| M3 | `all_reduce_perf` at 256 MiB and at the real gradient payload, N=5, both columns labelled | one command | Whether 0.75 GiB/s was algbw or busbw | busbw → the repo's all-reduce times are 1.6× too short and every overhead figure rises accordingly; algbw → the number is unreachable and must be re-measured |
| M4 | Count query heads per KV group in the planned config | one grep | The sign of the sparse-attention payoff | g ≥ 8 → vanilla NSA; g < 8 → FSA; g = 1 at 32k → vanilla NSA loses to dense |

The branch matrix:

| Branch | Link | TFLOPS/card | DDP overhead at 32k | Wall-clock for 9B | Frozen tables | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Gen2 x16 | 90 | ~1.5% | ~7 days | preferable, not necessary | DDP + frozen; skip P2P work |
| B | Gen2 x4 | 90 | ~7% | ~7 days | necessary (34–44% penalty otherwise) | DDP + frozen; skip P2P work |
| C | Gen1 x4 | 90 | ~11% | ~7 days | necessary (68–71% penalty otherwise) | DDP + frozen; PP becomes competitive |
| D | Gen2 x4 | 160 | ~10% | ~4 days | necessary (61% penalty) | DDP + frozen; raise global batch to 4M |

The recommendation — DDP, frozen tables, ≤1B trainable, 32k context, fine-block sparse attention rather than CSA2 — is optimal in all four branches. That is the report's real contribution. The conclusion is robust; the reasoning is not. In branch A it wins because the fabric is cheap and DDP is the simplest thing that works. In branch C it wins because even a 3× worse fabric costs only 11% at 32k, and PP's systems complexity is not worth the difference. In branch D it wins by a wider margin still. Four mutually exclusive premises, one recommendation.

What is *not* robust is everything downstream of the throughput denominator, and this is where the documentation's error compounds rather than merely misleads.

The wall-clock correction does not just change the schedule. It invalidates the statistical design. Nine billion tokens at 500M parameters is 18 tokens per parameter — 90% of Chinchilla-optimal, which is to say *at the knee* of the loss curve, the steepest and most transient part, where architecture differences are largest and least predictive [T3, [[training-compute-optimal-large-language-models]]]. The median published ablation budget in the corpus is 100B tokens, roughly 11× the box's run; the box sits at about the 15th percentile [T3]. And the two published studies nearest that budget are the two that report the smallest architecture effects in their families: MoBA's smallest scaling-law point (568M parameters, 10.8B tokens) is where its 32k penalty is largest and least asymptotic — a 0.105-nat gap that narrows to 0.045 at its largest point, a 2.3× swing inside one paper [T3, [[moba-mixture-of-block-attention-for]]]. The noise floor is a 3.5% relative restart spread, and nothing below ~4% relative is treated as a real modelling win [T3, [[a-hitchhikers-guide-to-scaling-law-estimation]]]. At the box's planned compute, MoBA's own fit predicts a ~0.060-nat gap — 2.2% relative, *below* the noise floor [T4]. So a 9B-token sparse-versus-dense comparison at 32k would be expected to return a null, and that null would be consistent with a real, small, asymptotic penalty.

The same arithmetic applies to the memory axis. Engram's own allocation optimum — its headline design law — buys 0.0139 nats at 993M active parameters and ~100B tokens [T3]. Memory Grafting's loss delta at 0.92B is within ±0.02 nats over 10–50B tokens. Neither is reported in a unit in which a noise floor has been measured, which is a separate problem, but neither is large.

So the box can afford exactly one run, at a budget that cannot resolve the effect it is trying to measure. The fix is not more compute — the compute is what it is, and the throughput correction has already spent it. The fix is a different metric and a smaller question. [[datadecide-how-to-predict-best-pretraining-data-with-small-experiments]] shows that decision accuracy is governed by the spread-to-noise ratio of the metric, not by compute, and that "the switch of metric allows small models to get above the noise floor for these tasks," taking code-task decision accuracy "from trivial to 80%" [T3]. The lesson transfers: choose a metric with high spread across the arms you are comparing, and a single 9B-token run per arm becomes informative. Choose mean validation loss, and it does not.

Two smaller items deserve to be on the record. The pretraining-variance work shows that floating-point arithmetic order — induced by sharding topology, gradient accumulation depth, or precision — produces functional divergence within 10–17% of changing the data seed, and that average validation loss cannot distinguish the two [T3, [[quantifying-pretraining-variance-data-initialization-and-floating-point-arithmet]]]. On a box where the sharding topology is a variable you are choosing, that is a floor on how much of any measured delta is attributable to the topology rather than the architecture. And the effective-learning-rate result shows that loss trajectories collapse when the learning rate divided by parameter norm is matched, which means any comparison across arms with different normalization schemes needs the ELR schedule matched, not just the learning rate [T3, [[effective-learning-rate-governs-loss-dynamics-in-language-model-pretraining]]].

The recommendation, then, in one paragraph. Run M1 through M4 first; they cost a morning and they convert every number in the documentation from a guess into a measurement. Then build the frozen-table DDP arm at ≤1B trainable and 32k context, with fine-block sparse attention (NSA, or FSA if the GQA group size is below 8) rather than CSA2, and with a global batch of 4M tokens rather than 1M — you are 17× below critical batch size, and the larger batch buys four times fewer all-reduces for free. Budget seven days per arm, not twenty-five hours, and plan for one arm, not four. Choose the metric before choosing the arm. And do not spend money on P2P patches or capacitor rework: P2P is 8% slower than host-staged for large copies and costs 26% on TP4 decode, and the capacitor mod changes width only, which at 32k buys you a six-percentage-point reduction in an overhead that is already single-digit.

## Source Tensions

Six tensions run through the material. Each has two defensible sides, and in each case the resolution is a measurement rather than an argument.

**Width is not speed.** [[physical-modsmd]] holds that link width and PCIe generation are independent axes that must never be conflated — the capacitor mod changes width only, the unlocker changes generation only, and a cap-modded card with no unlocker reports x16 at PCIe 1.0. The repo holds that the box is "PCIe Gen2 x4," stated as a single fact, with every bandwidth figure derived from it. Both are defensible and they are not in conflict: the repo's premise is *under-specified*, not wrong. "Gen2 x4" is a compound, the four states span 8× in one-way bandwidth, and the under-specification is the source of the 1.53× ceiling violation in the all-reduce arithmetic. M1 resolves it in one command. The reconciliation is that the repo's number is plausible as busbw and implausible as algbw, which means the repo is probably right about the fabric and wrong about the column.

**The club applies a stricter evidence standard to its own numbers than the repo applies to its.** [[club-170hxdocsbenchmarksmd-at-main-pixelmlclub-170hx-github]] states the rule explicitly: "A row is publication-safe only when the full sanitized receipt chain (manifest, commands, redacted outputs, exact model/runtime pins) is merged in the owning model repository." The repo treats user-reported figures — 0.75 GiB/s, 162 TFLOPS, ~70× EP cost — as measured facts carrying the quantitative spine of the program. By the club's own published rule, those three numbers are pending evidence repair, not decision-grade. This is not a rhetorical point; it is the corpus's own standard applied consistently. The resolution is to adopt the tier vocabulary and demote the repo's figures from measured to user-reported, which is what this report does throughout.

**Effect sizes are reported in three incompatible units.** [[memory-grafting-scaling-language-model]] reports the memory axis in benchmark score points (45.62 → 46.98 at 0.92B; 51.95 → 53.86 at 2.8B). [[moba-mixture-of-block-attention-for]] reports the attention axis in nats of trailing LM loss (0.105 → 0.045 across its sweep). [[a-hitchhikers-guide-to-scaling-law-estimation]] supplies a 3.5%-relative restart spread as the noise floor. These are not commensurable, and comparing them as if they were is the error. Score points on a ten-task average are not nats of training loss, and neither maps cleanly onto a relative-percentage floor. The sharper and more honest finding is not "the deltas are below noise" but "**no published Engram or CSA2 delta is reported in a unit in which a noise floor has been measured.**" That is a gap in the literature, not a verdict on the architectures, and it is the reason the metric choice matters more than the token budget.

**Every sparse-attention 32k win is an adaptation-stage result.** [[native-sparse-attention-hardware-aligned-and-natively]] pretrained 270B tokens at 8k, then continued training and SFT on 32k texts with YaRN. [[longcat-sparse-attention-taming-the-lightning-via]]'s 0.83×-at-32K figure comes from an appendix comparison, and its abstract's "native training to 1M" is a capability claim, not a from-scratch 32k efficiency claim. The repo plans from-scratch 32k training from step one, with no adaptation stage. Both sides are right about their own regime, and the repo's plan sits outside the regime every published result was measured in. The resolution is to separate the two claims: kernel efficiency transfers, quality does not, and the repo should cite only the former.

**P2P has two incompatible data-movement figures and the larger one is unverified.** One fork reports P2P working across all pairs at the one-way Gen2 x4 link rate of 1.68 GB/s each way [T2]. [[p2pmd]] reports that P2P is absent by default and that the one patch that could enable it half-works — data movement at 6.25 GB/s with synchronization broken, unverified, on one rig. A third measurement on a four-card PLX rig reports 5.79 GB/s on 12/12 verified pairs at Gen2 x16 [T2]. All three are consistent once the link state is separated: 1.68 GB/s is the x4 one-way rate, so P2P at x4 removes a traversal but cannot exceed the link; 5.79 GB/s is at x16 and is verified; 6.25 GB/s is at x16 and is not. The decisive fact is the one nobody cites: on that same PLX rig, host-staged copy moves large buffers at 6.27 GB/s, so P2P is 8% *slower* above 16 MiB, and TP4 decode loses 26% with P2P on. P2P is a latency lever, and the repo is right to skip it — for a better reason than it gives.

**The corpus's most trustworthy sustained numbers are at 180 W, not the repo's 250 W.** The club's decision-grade benchmarks run against an enforced 180.00 W limit, and the 74.6–78.5 TFLOP/s figure is a ten-minute `gpu-burn -tc` at that cap [T2]. The repo carries 162 TFLOPS per card at a 250 W stock cap, and [[performancemd]]'s 164.4–192.7 band is a burst GEMM peak, not a sustained training rate [T2]. The 74.6–78.5 figure is a **floor**, not a ceiling — at 250 W the sustained number should be higher, but nobody has measured it. The honest training denominator is therefore a range, 75–100 TFLOPS per card, with the upper end unmeasured. M2 settles it, and until M2 runs, every wall-clock estimate on this box carries a 1.3× error bar from that axis alone.
