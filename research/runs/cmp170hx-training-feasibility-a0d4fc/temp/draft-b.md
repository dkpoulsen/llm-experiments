# Draft B — The Fabric Binds Through Availability, Not Cost

**Thesis.** The repo is right that the PCIe fabric is the binding constraint on this box, and right that DDP plus frozen Engram tables is the correct response — but it is right for reasons it never states. The fabric does not make DDP expensive; DDP overhead at 1M-token global batches is roughly 7%, not the repo's 36%. What the fabric does is make every *other* topology unavailable: TP and EP are not costly on this box, they are absent, because no SM80 training kernel and no collective library exists that can run without NVLink, P2P or RDMA. That distinction — availability rather than cost — is what survives every correction in this corpus, and it is the only framing under which the repo's topology decision, its batch-size decision and its frozen-table decision all follow from one premise instead of three.

**What fails.** The repo's quantitative spine fails in ways that matter and in ways that do not. Its throughput prediction of 80–120k tok/s at 32k context is 2–3× above the hardware's theoretical maximum and is simply wrong; the honest band is 9–22k tok/s, which turns a planned 25-hour run into 5–12 days. Its ~70× expert-parallelism estimate is wrong by an order of magnitude. Its DDP overhead constant is wrong by 5×, in the safe direction. Its claim that frozen tables make a 7B-total model fit is true only if the tables total 12 GB rather than 12 GB per layer — the repo's own figure, read literally, breaks the repo's own claim.

**What holds.** The decision set does not change. Data-parallel training with large global batches, no tensor parallelism, no expert parallelism, frozen memory tables, and a sparse-attention substitution arm remains the only experiment program this box can run. The corrections change what the program will *cost* and what it can *prove*, not what it can *attempt*.

Evidence is labelled throughout: **T1** measured on this box (all user-reported; the corpus contains no on-box training, all-reduce, NCCL or DDP measurement at all), **T2** community or adjacent-hardware measurement, **T3** paper-measured, **T4** arithmetic from stated assumptions.

## 1. Hardware Reality: Link Width, Power Brake, and What the Numbers Assume

The repo states "PCIe Gen2 x4" as a single fact and derives every communication number from it. That is the most consequential under-specification in the program, because width and generation are physically independent axes that the community documentation separates explicitly. The capacitor mod changes link width only and never changes generation; the unlocker changes generation only and works at any width; a card with neither runs Gen1 x4. The fuse table that governs the width is clear, the lanes are depopulated, and `lspci` reports capability x16 against negotiated status x4 — four distinct link states, all reachable, all with different bandwidth [[p2pmd]]. The repo's own settings document states the fabric premise as a single line and never records which of the four states it was measured under [[deepseek-v4-cmp170hxsettingsmd-at-317dac12bbd7f401f0538c76161bc7186086fb65-allov]].

This matters because the four states span a 7× range. Measured one-way rates on this silicon are approximately 0.85 GB/s at Gen1 x4, 1.68 GB/s send and 1.71 GB/s receive at Gen2 x4, 2.88 GB/s at Gen1 x16, and 6.63–6.67 GB/s at Gen2 x16 — the last being about 83% of the 8 GB/s line rate, with an independent four-card capture at 5.97 GB/s per card and zero AER errors over ninety minutes [[performancemd]]. The Gen2 x4 figure is corroborated by a direct measurement on this exact hardware at this exact width: "800MB/s at Gen 1 and 1.6GB/s at Gen 2" [[exploration-of-cryptocurrency-mining-specific-gpus-in-ai]]. The repo's 0.75 GiB/s is therefore roughly 45–48% of the Gen2 x4 one-way rate — depending on whether its unit is GB or GiB — and roughly 95% of the Gen1 x4 rate. Both readings are live, and they point in opposite directions: if the box is at Gen1 x4, a fuse setting is leaving 2× on the table; if it is at Gen2 x4, then 0.75 GiB/s is a *software* ceiling sitting at less than half the link's capability.

That second reading is the one the corpus supports, and it is the single most important fact for the repo's defence. NCCL's host-staged path on PCIe-dependent systems is known to underperform its own performance model — transfers slow down when multiple transfers traverse the CPU in a specific pattern, bounce-buffer placement is poor, and these congestion patterns are invisible to libraries designed for links that bypass the CPU entirely. That is the stated reason a research collective library exists at all: "existing collective communication libraries do not achieve the target performance that their performance model estimates in PCIe-dependent systems" [[github-mcrltccl-thunder-research-groups-collective-communication-library-github]]. The best measured multi-rank busbw on adjacent hardware is 3.69–4.24 GB/s on three cards at Gen2 x16 behind a PLX switch with SHM transport and no P2P [[readmemd]] — roughly 53% of the 8 GB/s line rate. Apply that 53% efficiency to a Gen2 x4 link and you get ~0.85 GB/s busbw, which is within 13% of the repo's number. The repo did not measure a broken fabric. It measured NCCL being NCCL on a narrow link.

The compute side is in better shape than the corrections suggest. The repo carries ~162 TFLOPS bf16 per card and ~810 TFLOPS aggregate. Measured BF16 tensor throughput on unlocked cards at stock clocks spans 164.4–192.7 TFLOPS against a 202.1 TFLOPS arithmetic ceiling of 2048 FLOPs × 70 SM × 1410 MHz, and the printed specification sheet lists 49.05 TFLOPS in both its locked and unlocked columns — a figure that is 3.3× below what the silicon actually does [[performancemd]] [[full-specifications-170th-street]] [[specifications-cmp-170hx-documentation]]. The repo's 162 sits inside the measured band, at its low end. So the aggregate ~810 is conservative rather than wrong, and the correction to a 90–100 TFLOPS/card training denominator is a correction to a *different quantity*: sustained training throughput, not GEMM peak. The corpus's most trustworthy sustained number is 74.6–78.5 TFLOPS/card from a ten-minute `gpu-burn` at an enforced 180 W limit with no thermal throttle [[club-170hxdocscmp170hx-understandingmd-at-main-pixelmlclub-170hx-github]]. That is a floor, not a ceiling, because the card's stock cap is 250 W — and nobody has measured sustained BF16 training at 250 W. The honest range is 75–100 TFLOPS/card, with the upper end unmeasured.

The power brake is the one hardware hazard the repo handles correctly and the one that most threatens every number in this draft. The PWRBRK# edge pin, when asserted, silently caps the card at roughly 88 W and drops measured throughput to 39.3 TFLOPS against 155.7 TFLOPS on a healthy unlocked card at 250 W — a 4× collapse that presents as a bad GPU rather than a bad pin [[lessonsmd]]. Any benchmark run without a PWRBRK# check is a benchmark of unknown provenance. This is not a footnote: it is the mechanism by which a perfectly good box produces numbers that look like a fabric problem.

Two further calibration points bound the aggregate. The stock, unmodded card measures ~42 TFLOPS in a mixbench-style workload and throttles FMA to 0.39 TFLOPS, which is why the "all GB/s without FLOPS" framing exists [[all-gbs-without-flops-nvidia-cmp-170hx-review-performance-lockdown-workaround-te]]. And the A100 datasheet that the community uses as the scaling reference lists 312 TFLOPS bf16 dense on 108 SMs — so a 70-SM GA100-105F-A1 part at 1410 MHz should land near 202 TFLOPS arithmetic, exactly where the measured ceiling sits [[nvidia-a100-tensor-core-gpu-data-sheet-jun21-1]]. The repo's hardware table is defensible. Its *link* description is not.

The practical consequence: the repo's 0.75 GiB/s is not a measurement of the fabric. It is a measurement of NCCL on the fabric, taken under an unspecified link state, with no command, no message size, no algbw/busbw label and no repetition count [[interim-report-allreduce-ceiling-and-definition]]. By the community's own published rule — a row is publication-safe only when the full sanitized receipt chain is merged, and unmerged rows are "pending evidence repair" — the number is not decision-grade. But the absence of a receipt does not make it wrong. It makes it unfalsifiable, and the likely direction of the error is *against* the repo: the hardware ceiling is higher than 0.75 GiB/s, so the repo has probably been too pessimistic about its own fabric, not too optimistic.

## 2. Training Topologies on a No-P2P PCIe Fabric

The repo's headline topology claim is that DDP is the only viable option. That is the right answer reached by the wrong route, and the wrong route matters because it produces the wrong cost model downstream.

Start with what is genuinely unavailable. Tensor parallelism needs two all-reduces per transformer layer — 86 collectives on a 43-layer model — and is communication-bound at every sequence length on this fabric [[lessonsmd]]. At 1M-token global batches the payload per all-reduce is on the order of gigabytes and the collective count is in the dozens, which puts TP at roughly 28× the compute window by the corpus's own arithmetic [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]]. Expert parallelism needs two all-to-alls per MoE layer, and here the constraint is not arithmetic but software: every MoE-specialised EP library in existence — DeepEP, HybridEP, pplx-kernels, NCCL EP, NIXL — assumes GPUs can directly access one another through NVLink, GPUDirect P2P or RDMA, and that assumption is "fundamental to both their data transfer and synchronization mechanisms". On consumer-class GPUs without P2P, "existing EP communication libraries cannot operate on these systems" [[efficient-expert-parallel-communication]]. Megatron-Core's dispatcher menu makes the same point from the production side: AllGather is "simple but memory-intensive; suitable for small EP sizes", all-to-all is the standard NCCL path, and the high-performance Flex path — DeepEP and HybridEP — is explicitly for "NVLink-rich topologies like NVL72" [[scalable-training-of-mixture-of-experts-models-with]]. On this box, EP=5 with AllGather is the only legal configuration, and AllGather is the one the production framework itself labels memory-intensive.

Now the part the repo gets wrong. Pipeline parallelism is viable, and for a 7B-total model it is arguably better than DDP. PP=5 fits at ~25.8 GB/card and pays only a 4–14% bubble, because its 134 MB per-micro-batch activation hand-off hides inside a 243 ms compute window [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]]. The hand-off is point-to-point rather than collective, and on a no-P2P fabric point-to-point is staged through host memory — but at 134 MB and 1.68 GB/s that is ~80 ms, comfortably inside the compute window. PP is the topology the repo dismissed without arithmetic, and it is the one that solves the memory problem DDP cannot [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]].

FSDP and ZeRO-3 are technically runnable but pay 378% overhead because they move 1.5× DDP's bytes per layer rather than 2×, and the per-layer all-gather/reduce-scatter pattern multiplies the collective count by the layer count [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]] [[zero-3-vs-fsdp-memory-efficiency-for-llm-training-lyceum]]. The deeper problem with ZeRO-class sharding on this box is not bandwidth at all. On eight A100s with NVSwitch — a fabric with roughly 350× this box's link rate — ZeRO-2 delivered 28.7 samples/s against DDP's 83.1, and every overlap knob produced zero change (3,071 → 3,093 ms). The ~2.4 s delta was CPU-side per-parameter Python hooks, which is fabric-independent and would bite identically here, worst on models with many small tensors — that is, MoE experts [[perf-29-throughput-by-switching-from-deepspeed-to-ddp-issue-177-tensorautoopen-2]]. This is the single most under-appreciated fact in the topology question: the reason to prefer DDP on this box is not that the fabric punishes sharding. It is that the *Python* punishes sharding, and the fabric is innocent.

The adjacent-hardware record is consistent with that reading. Ten RTX 3090s sharing ~3 GB/s of PCIe achieve only 19% DDP scaling efficiency at ten GPUs [[pcie-110-gpu-스케일링]]; RoundPipe reports that ZeRO spends roughly 70% of training time on communication on consumer PCIe and beats its baselines by 1.48–2.16× on eight RTX 4090s by attacking weight-binding bubbles rather than bandwidth [[efficient-training-on-multiple-consumer-gpus-with]]; and Mobius demonstrates that pipeline parallelism on commodity GPU servers is a workable answer when the interconnect is the limit [[mobius-fine-tuning-large-scale-models-on]]. A general distributed-training reference implementation reaches the same conclusion from the practitioner side, treating DDP as the default and sharding as the exception [[distributed-trainingreadmemd-at-main-cortwavedistributed-training-github]].

P2P deserves a paragraph because the repo treats its absence as a constraint and the evidence says it is closer to a non-event. BAR1 P2P on four CMP 170HX cards over two PLX switches at Gen2 x16 measures 256 MiB all-reduce at N=2: SHM 3.69 → P2P 4.83 GB/s (+31%); at N=3: SHM 4.24 → P2P 3.86 GB/s (−9%). On a real workload, GLM-5.3-Flash TP4 decode goes from 19.4 ms/step with P2P off to 26.1 ms/step with P2P on, a 25–44% regression [[2026-10-01-cmp170hx-4card-bar1-p2p-plx-cudaipynb]]. At Gen2 x4 with P2P working, "at Gen2-x4 bandwidth no row shows a strict win" and PP4 loses 19.1% aggregate [[readme_enmd]]. P2P is a bandwidth lever only at N=2 on wide links; on a five-rank Gen2 x4 fabric it is at best neutral and at worst a pessimization [[2026-10-01-cmp170hx-4card-bar1-p2p-plx-cudaipynb]]. The community wiki's own P2P page records the half-working patch — peer data movement at 6.25 GB/s with peer synchronisation broken, which would leave NCCL hanging — and the three-card no-P2P baseline at Gen2 x16 that already saturates the link [[p2pmd]] [[readmemd]]. The repo's "no P2P" is therefore not the constraint it thinks it is — which, again, cuts in the repo's favour: one of its three stated fabric disabilities turns out to be irrelevant.

| Topology | Collectives per step | Dominant cost | Overhead vs compute window | Fits 7B on 64 GB? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DDP | 1 all-reduce | 2.0 GB bf16 grads | ~6.6% (T4, corrected window) | No (112 GB/card) | Viable for ≤1B trainable |
| ZeRO-2 | reduce-scatter + all-gather | DDP-identical 2Ψ bytes | ~6.6% bandwidth + CPU hooks (T2) | Yes (33.6 GB state) | Best memory/comm trade |

The table's shape is the argument. DDP and ZeRO-2 are cheap; PP is cheap and solves memory; TP and EP are not expensive so much as impossible. The repo's "DDP only" should be restated as "**data-parallel only**, with ZeRO-2 as the memory fix that costs nothing in communication and PP=5 as the fallback that costs nothing in memory". That is a materially different recommendation from the one the repo makes, and it is derived entirely from the same fabric premise.

## 3. The DDP Overhead Model at 1M-Token Batches

The repo's model is a single constant: overhead = 3.6 × 10⁵ / global_batch_tokens, giving 36% at 1M tokens and 18% at 2M. Two independent corrections hit the two halves of that ratio, and they push in opposite directions.

The numerator correction. If 0.75 GiB/s is the nccl-tests *busbw* column rather than algbw, then at N=5 the busbw-to-algbw ratio is 2(N−1)/N = 1.6, so the effective ring rate is 0.469 GB/s and the communication numerator is 1.6× larger than the repo assumed. That puts DDP overhead at 54–58% at 1M tokens [[interim-report-allreduce-ceiling-and-definition]]. The inference is not from the user's command output — it is from the mutual exclusivity of "no P2P" and 0.75 GiB/s read as algbw, since a host-staged ring on this link cannot exceed the one-way rate. That is a reasonable inference and it is still an inference.

The denominator correction. 6ND is invalid at 32k context. PaLM's own accounting is 6N + 12LHQT, and at 32k with a 1B-active model attention is 88% of the step and total FLOPs are 8.5× the 6ND estimate [[interim-report-sustained-throughput-at-32k]]. The compute window at 1M tokens is therefore not 7.4 s but ~56.8 s. Simultaneously the defensible training denominator is 90–100 TFLOPS/card rather than 162, which cuts the other way but is swamped by the 8.5×.

Put them together and the ratio collapses. At ~0.53 GB/s algbw, 2.0 GB of bf16 gradients takes 3.8 s; over a 56.8 s window that is **6.6%**, or 13.2% with fp32 buckets [[interim-report-allreduce-ceiling-and-definition]]. The repo's 36% is wrong by roughly 5×, and it is wrong in the direction that makes the fabric look more binding than it is. The algbw-versus-busbw question, which looked like the decisive dispute, moves the answer by about 4.4 percentage points either way once the compute window is corrected. It is a narrative question, not a quantitative one.

This is the most important structural finding in the whole assessment, and it deserves to be stated plainly: **the repo's conclusion survives while both of its premises fail.** DDP overhead at 1M-token batches is small, large global batches are the right lever, and the fabric is not what limits the run. But the repo got there by multiplying a wrong numerator by a wrong denominator, and the errors nearly cancelled. A program built on a number that is right by accident is a program that cannot tell when it has stopped being right [[interim-report-allreduce-ceiling-and-definition]].

The throughput prediction is where the accident stops being benign. The repo predicts 80–120k tok/s for a ~500M dense baseline at 32k. The theoretical maximum at 32k is 39.6k tok/s at 100% MFU; the realistic band is 9–22k tok/s. So 9B tokens takes 5–12 days, not the planned 25 hours [[interim-report-sustained-throughput-at-32k]]. This is a pure FLOPs error, entirely independent of the fabric, and it is the largest single correction in the corpus.

There is a reconciliation available, and it is worth stating because it shows the repo had the right measurement and applied it to the wrong context length. The repo's own 494M-model run at ~140k tok/s implies 6 × 494M × 140k = 4.15 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s across five cards, or ~83 TFLOPS/card — a plausible 43–51% MFU against the 162–192.7 TFLOPS band. That is a *training-shaped* measurement, the only one in the corpus, and it supports a high MFU at short context. Scale it to 32k by the 8.5× attention factor and 140k tok/s becomes ~16.5k tok/s, which lands squarely inside the 9–22k band. The repo's throughput number is not fabricated. It is a short-context number wearing a 32k label [[interim-report-sustained-throughput-at-32k]].

The batch-size decision has a similar shape. A 1M-token global batch is only token-efficient once the critical batch size exceeds 1M tokens, and critical batch size is not a constant — it is near zero at initialization, rises rapidly, and then plateaus, and this trajectory holds across 1B and 7B model sizes [[critical-batch-size-revisited-a-simple-empirical]]. For OLMo 1B, the critical batch size reaches 2048 sequences only after 168B tokens and 4096 sequences only after 503B tokens. At the repo's 9B-token budget, the critical batch size is far below 1M tokens, so a 1M-token batch from step one sits above the critical point and burns token efficiency for wall-clock that the box cannot deliver anyway. The gradient-noise-scale framework predicts linear parallelization only up to the noise scale, with training speed falling to 50% of maximum at that point [[an-empirical-model-of-large-batch-training]]. And the compute-optimal frontier itself says a 1B model should see roughly 20B tokens, not 9B — so the token budget is short of the compute-optimal ratio before any architecture question is asked [[training-compute-optimal-large-language-models]].

But here the fabric and the batch-size literature agree, which is the opposite of what the repo assumes. Collective payload scales with batch size, so a *small* global batch is *cheap* on this fabric and a large one is expensive. Batch-size warmup — start small, grow as the critical batch size grows — is therefore cheaper on Gen2 x4 than a fixed 1M-token batch from step one, not more expensive. The repo's 1M-token target is right as a steady-state destination. Its "from step one" is wrong, and correcting it makes the fabric cost go *down*.

One more measurement artifact belongs here. Five- and seven-GPU runs show "much lower" bandwidth utilization than four or eight, and the mechanism is offset alignment — 4- or 8-byte offsets instead of 16 — which is payload-shape-dependent and paddable away in DDP gradient buckets [[why-is-the-bandwidth-utilization-of-7-gpus-much-lower-than-that-of-6-8-gpus-for]]. A five-rank box is exactly the awkward count, so the N=5 penalty is real. It is a CPU-flow artifact, not a link-bandwidth collapse, and it is fixable in software.

## 4. MoE and Expert Parallelism: Ruled Out or Misjudged?

The repo estimates expert parallelism at ~70× the cost of dense training and rules it out on that basis. The arithmetic does not support 70×. Megatron-Core gives the per-GPU send volume in each all-to-all as approximately T·K·h·EP⁻¹, with a full dispatch-and-combine cycle doubling it [[scalable-training-of-mixture-of-experts-models-with]]. At 1M tokens, top-2 routing, hidden 2048 and EP=5, that is ~1.64 GB per GPU per all-to-all, ~3.3 GB per MoE layer for the cycle, and on the order of 79 GB per step across 24 MoE layers — about 47 s at 0.53 GB/s against a 56.8 s window, or roughly 6× once aggregate volume is divided by GPU count rather than left un-divided [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]]. The repo divided aggregate volume by per-GPU bandwidth, which is where the extra order of magnitude came from.

So EP is expensive, not astronomically expensive. What actually rules it out is availability, and the production literature says so in its own vocabulary. The communication wall is described as a function of *where* the traffic goes: "as EP grows, this volume saturates but the communication increasingly moves from high-bandwidth intra-node links (e.g., NVLink) to narrower inter-node interconnects, where available bandwidth drops by an order of magnitude". On NVLink-rich multi-node DeepSeek-V3, "unoptimized all-to-all can consume up to 60% of total training time" [[scalable-training-of-mixture-of-experts-models-with]]. That 60% is the number to hold onto. It is measured on a fabric where the drop is one order of magnitude. On this box the drop from NVLink's 600 GB/s to Gen2 x4's 1.68 GB/s is roughly 350×, not 10×. Sixty percent of training time on a fabric 35× better than this one is not a cost estimate; it is a wall.

The honest concession is that EP on PCIe is not physically impossible, and two papers show it. ThunderEP replaces the multi-step ring with a single-step collective, moves bulk data on DMA engines, and limits host-memory polling, achieving average speedups of 2.00× and 1.53× over NCCL for dispatch and combine on RTX 4090 and RTX 5090 PCIe systems, with up to 1.66× end-to-end [[efficient-expert-parallel-communication]]. CoMoE elevates the host to an active routing hub, uses host-backed token multicast and token-level aggregation, and reaches 85–90% of the throughput of the strongest NVLink-capable A800 configuration at 23.4% of the hardware cost [[democratizing-moe-inference-on-commodity-gpus]]. VarioPath adds a topology-aware offline analyzer and an online demand-aware scheduler, reporting average AlltoAllv speedups of 5.88× over FAST and 1.72× over DeepEP across four platforms up to 256 GPUs [[variopath-workload-aware-all-to-all-communication-for-pcie-gpu-clusters]].

All three are inference systems. None has a training-grade backward pass, none was evaluated at Gen2 x4, and the fastest of them runs on PCIe 5.0 x16 at roughly 55 GB/s — about 32× this box's link. So the correct statement of the repo's claim is narrower and stronger than the one it makes: **EP is ruled out for training on this box not by arithmetic but by the absence of a single SM80 training-grade EP implementation**, and the inference literature demonstrates that the fabric is surmountable by exactly the kind of specialised kernel work the repo has no plan to do.

There is a second, more interesting reason to avoid MoE here, and the repo does not state it. MoE's purpose is to decouple parameter count from per-token FLOPs, which lets you buy capacity with memory. On a five-card box with 320 GB of HBM, memory is the scarce resource and FLOPs are comparatively abundant. MoE therefore spends the scarce resource to save the abundant one. Worse, MoE multiplies DDP's communication by the total-to-active ratio: a 7B-total/1B-active model all-reduces 7B parameters while computing only 1B, so its DDP overhead is 7× a dense model's — 252% under the repo's own model, ~46% under the corrected window [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]]. MoE is the wrong axis for this box [[interim-report-topology-cost-at-1m-tokens]]. The frozen-table design is the right axis precisely because it buys capacity with memory that is *frozen*, and frozen memory costs neither optimizer state nor a collective.

## 5. Engram and Frozen Memory: Making 7B Fit on 64 GB

The repo's claim is that freezing the Engram memory tables makes a 7B-total/~1B-trainable model fit DDP on 64 GB cards. The claim is true, but the repo's own arithmetic for it does not close, and the reason it does not close is the most useful thing in this section.

Start with what the paper actually specifies. For each n-gram order, the top 1M most frequent n-grams are selected and their frozen memory representations computed offline, yielding 3M entries per grafted layer; each entry stores a hidden-state vector in bfloat16, and for a 35B-A3B grafting model this corresponds to roughly 12 GB of frozen memory per layer [[memory-grafting-scaling-language-model]]. The repo carries 33M entries at 12 GB per layer. Those two figures cannot both be right: 33M entries at a 2048-wide hidden state in bf16 is ~135 GB, not 12 GB. The repo's table is either 11× larger than the paper's largest tested table at the same byte count, or its byte count is per-model rather than per-layer. Read literally — 12 GB per layer — a three-layer Engram stack is 36 GB of frozen tables, and 36 GB of tables plus ~16 GB of trainable state plus ~20 GB of 32k activations is 72 GB, which does not fit on a 64 GB card. The repo's own numbers break the repo's own claim.

Read the other way — 12 GB total — everything closes: 12 GB of frozen tables, 16 GB of trainable state (2 GB bf16 weights, 2 GB gradients, 8 GB Adam moments, 4 GB fp32 master weights for 1B trainable parameters), and ~20 GB of activations at 32k with a 1M-token global batch gives ~48 GB/card. That fits with headroom. So the design is sound and the specification is off by a factor of three.

Now the part the repo never says, which is the real justification. Engram's own training recipe shards the tables across GPUs and uses All-to-All to gather active rows in the forward pass and dispatch gradients in the backward pass [[dr-robert-li-engram-a-critical-analysis-of-conditional-memory-as-a-sparsity-axis]]. That is precisely the collective this fabric cannot run — the same all-to-all that rules out expert parallelism in §4. Freezing the tables does not merely save memory. It *removes the collective entirely*: no all-to-all, no gradient dispatch, no optimizer state, no per-step table update. The frozen-table design is not a memory trick that happens to help; it is the only Engram variant that keeps the architecture off the wire. That is the strongest available form of the repo's claim, and it is a fabric argument, not a memory argument.

The paper supports this from the other direction. "Empirically, training efficiency began to decline once the memory module accounts for more than 70% of total parameters" [[memory-grafting-scaling-language-model]]. Trainable Engram tables do not scale — the paper's own finding is that past a threshold you must stop training them. The repo's design is the paper's conclusion, not a deviation from it. The same paper reports that offloading a 100B-parameter memory costs around 2% end-to-end inference throughput on a 4B-dense backbone, and the underlying Engram work reports <3% overhead for offloading 100B parameters to host DRAM (8,858 vs 9,032 tokens/sec) [[dr-robert-li-engram-a-critical-analysis-of-conditional-memory-as-a-sparsity-axis]]. Host-resident frozen tables are a solved problem at inference; extending that to training is a matter of prefetch scheduling, because frozen tables are read-only and need no gradient path.

That extension has a fabric price, and it is worth computing because it is exactly the kind of cost the repo's framing predicts. If the 6B frozen parameters (12 GB in bf16) live in host DRAM rather than HBM, each token retrieves one entry per Engram layer — 4 KB at a 2048-wide hidden state — so a 1M-token step across three Engram layers touches ~12 GB of PCIe traffic. At 1.68 GB/s that is 7.1 s against a 56.8 s window, or ~12.5% overhead (T4). If the tables are replicated in HBM instead, the PCIe cost is zero and the HBM cost is 12 GB/card, which still fits. So the design has two working configurations and one non-working one, and the repo specifies the non-working one [[memory-grafting-scaling-language-model]].

The memory arithmetic also shows the repo's claim is not the *only* way to fit 7B. A fully trainable 7B model with Adam needs ~112 GB/card under plain DDP and does not fit. But ZeRO-2 at five ranks needs 33.6 GB of sharded optimizer state with DDP-identical 2Ψ communication and preserves Adam; Adafactor needs 28 GB; 8-bit Adam needs 42 GB [[interim-report-memory-budget-and-frozen-tables]]. So freezing the tables is an architecture choice, not a memory necessity. The repo reached a defensible conclusion by conflating three different things: the memory problem (solvable by ZeRO-2), the communication problem (solvable by staying data-parallel), and the architecture problem (solvable only by freezing).

| Variant | Trainable | Optimizer state | Frozen tables | Activations @32k | Total/card | Fits 64 GB? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7B fully trainable, DDP + Adam | 7B | 84 GB | 0 | ~20 GB | ~112 GB | No (T4) |
| 7B fully trainable, ZeRO-2 ×5 | 7B | 33.6 GB sharded | 0 | ~20 GB | ~54 GB | Yes (T4) |
| 7B fully trainable, Adafactor | 7B | 28 GB | 0 | ~20 GB | ~48 GB | Yes (T4) |

The last row is the one to watch. The paper's own 2.8B configuration uses a 12 GB-per-layer table, and at three or more grafted layers that exceeds a single card before any activations are counted. The paper trains it, so it must shard or offload — which means the paper's own recipe reintroduces the collective the repo is trying to avoid. The repo's design is a genuine improvement on the paper for this fabric, and it should say so.

The measurement problem is separate and cannot be engineered away. Memory Grafting moves the 0.92B average benchmark score from 45.62 (MoE) and 45.03 (vanilla Engram) to 46.98, and the 2.8B score from 51.95/52.43 to 53.86 [[memory-grafting-scaling-language-model]]. Those are score points on a ten-task average, not nats of training loss, and no published Engram or CSA2 delta is reported in a unit in which a noise floor has been established. A 9B-token run therefore cannot detect the effect it is designed to detect — not because the effect is small, but because the effect has never been expressed in the unit the run will measure. That is a stronger and more falsifiable claim than "underpowered": it is settled by a single experiment that reports the delta in nats with two seeds [[interim-report-token-budget-and-effect-size]]. Published ablations in this space run at 50–350B tokens, and the repo's 9B-token budget is one to two orders of magnitude below them.

The fix is not a bigger run. It is a different shape of run. Fitting scaling laws to intermediate checkpoints rather than final losses substantially improves accuracy, estimates are most accurate when derived from models of similar size, and because there is significant variability across seeds, "training multiple small models is sometimes more useful than training a single large one" — it is often preferable to extrapolate from many small, partially trained models than from a few large ones [[a-hitchhikers-guide-to-scaling-law-estimation]]. A five-card box is precisely the machine that can afford twenty small runs instead of one large one. The box's smallness is an advantage for the measurement problem the repo actually has.

## 6. CSA2 Sparse Attention at 32k Context

The repo's claim is that CSA2 compressed sparse attention pays off at 32k training context. It decomposes into a feasibility claim and a payoff claim, and they have different answers.

Feasibility first. The gate on sequence length is the indexer, not the attention kernel. Public CSA implementations materialise a `[B, S, H_I, T]` FP32 score tensor before the top-k reduction; with H_I=64 indexer heads and a compression ratio m=4, that intermediate is 256 GB at S=65,536 and 64 GiB at S=32,768 — exceeding any single-GPU HBM budget [[streamindex-memory-bounded-compressed-sparse]]. A chunked partition-merge top-k driver that never materialises the full intermediate runs the same indexer to S=1,048,576 with 6.21 GB peak HBM, a 32× regime extension, with set-overlap recall bit-exact at small S and mean recall rounding to 1.0000 across three design-space sweeps [[streamindex-memory-bounded-compressed-sparse]]. At 32k the chunked path costs ~0.40 GB and is 10.3× faster than the materialise path [[interim-report-csa2-at-32k-sm80]]. So the indexer fits, and the arithmetic for it is solid.

The attention kernel is a different story. The production sparse-attention forward kernel is C++ and is not integrated into the cuDNN DSA module; the module's kernels target Hopper and Blackwell, with dense indexer forward and standalone indexer top-k supporting both architectures and the combined compressed-logits + top-k path SM100-only [[deepseek-sparse-attention-dsa-nvidia-cudnn]]. The pure-PyTorch reference implementation exists for numerical verification and is explicitly "uncompiled, slow" [[dsa_referencepy]]. The Ampere port of FlashMLA exposes only paged-KV decode, has no backward, and reports that "the performance of Ampere is currently poor due to register overflow" — 464 GB/s and 59 TFLOPS on A100 SXM against 2039 GB/s and 312 TFLOPS peak, or 23% of bandwidth and 19% of FLOPS [[github-pzhao-engflashmla-github]]. The upstream tracker confirms that "the sparse attention implementation appears to be forward-only", with a commenter noting that sparse backward needs atomic accumulation over misaligned K/V and would take months of CUDA work [[missing-sparse-backward-kernel-issue-107-deepseek-aiflashmla-github]]. SGLang has "no end-to-end CUDA path for running DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) on NVIDIA A100 / SM80", blocked independently by DeepGEMM's absence on SM80 and by FA3's inability to handle the asymmetric 576/512 head dimensions — and the feature request was closed as inactive without anyone building it [[feature-add-an-opt-in-sm80-fallback-for-deepseek-sparse-attention-dsa-on-nvidia]].

So the repo's claim (d) is feasible only in a narrow sense: the *substitution arm* is runnable on SM80, because FSA is "well tested with NVIDIA Ampere or Hopper GPUs (e.g., A100 SXM)", supports training and prefill, and backprops through a 65,536 + 32,768-token batch; and the FLA repository ships full NSA training via `flame` [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2026]] [[github-fla-orgnative-sparse-attention-efficient-triton-implementations-for-nativ]]. But *CSA2 specifically* is not runnable on SM80 without writing the backward kernel yourself. That is a kernel project measured in months, not a configuration change, and the repo's plan does not budget for it [[interim-report-csa2-at-32k-sm80]].

Payoff second, and here the repo is roughly half right. LongCat's Table 2 measures core-attention latency under a representative *training* setting — batch size 1, query length 8192, BF16, top-k 2048 — and at 32K the hardware-friendly variant moves forward from 23.64 to 19.70 ms and backward from 238.60 to 137.77 ms, a total of 262.24 → 157.47 ms, or 1.67× on core attention [[longcat-sparse-attention-taming-the-lightning-via]]. That is a real training-shaped win at exactly the repo's target context. But the same paper reports that once indexer overhead is included, full-layer speedup "becomes increasingly constrained by indexing cost, reaching a peak of 1.49× for prefill at 4K context and 1.14× for decode at 8K context, before gradually diminishing to 1.02–1.04× at longer contexts". At 1024K the indexer accounts for up to 90% of per-layer latency, and indexer latency grows 27× from 0.034 ms at 4K to 0.930 ms at 1024K while the sparse-attention kernel stays flat at ~0.10 ms [[longcat-sparse-attention-taming-the-lightning-via]]. Attention is also only part of a layer. So the end-to-end training payoff at 32k is small — plausibly 1.0–1.1×, not the 2× the repo implies.

The independent measurement agrees on the magnitude and adds a warning about the sign. FSA reports up to 1.25× and on average 1.09× over NSA, and up to 2.47× and on average 1.86× over full attention; dividing the two averages gives NSA ≈ 1.71× full attention end-to-end [[published-as-a-conference-paper-at-iclr-2026]]. That is inside the 2× threshold, and roughly half the committed size. More importantly: "vanilla NSA lags behind full attention in many tested cases… when the sequence length is 32K, one GQA group contains one query head, NSA consistently falls short of full attention." The FLA Triton kernels corroborate the shape — 2.66× forward and 1.76× backward at 16,384, 1.35× and 1.06× at 8,192, and *slower* than flash at 4,096 (0.72× forward, 0.61× backward) [[github-fla-orgnative-sparse-attention-efficient-triton-implementations-for-nativ]]. Sparse attention's sign at 32k depends on the GQA group size, and that configuration is the decisive unmeasured variable. If the repo's 494–500M baseline has g=1 at 32k, the substitution arm is a net loss.

The steelman case for the repo's claim rests on memory rather than FLOPs, and it is strong. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash combines cross-layer KV cache reuse in CSA2 with FP4 KV caching to reduce its global KV cache footprint to 890 bytes per token, roughly 1/4 of DeepSeek-V4-Flash and 437× below DeepSeek-V1, and it trains sparse attention from scratch at a sequence length of 64K with no dense attention warmup stages on a 45T-token corpus [[deepseek-v41-flash]]. That last clause is the direct receipt for the repo's from-scratch 32k plan: a frontier lab does exactly this, at 64K, at scale. And the memory consequence is decisive for this box. At a 1M-token global batch, 890 bytes per token is ~890 MB of KV cache; a full-attention MLA configuration at ~35 KB per token is ~35 GB. That difference is the difference between a batch that fits across five 64 GB cards and one that does not. CSA2 at 32k pays off on this box — as a memory enabler, not a speedup. The repo is right about the decision and wrong about the mechanism.

Two further pieces of the design space belong here because they are cheaper than CSA2 and achieve the same memory goal. YOCO's decoder-decoder architecture caches key-value pairs once and reuses them via cross-attention, reducing KV memory and prefill latency while retaining global attention, and extends to 1M context with near-perfect needle retrieval [[you-only-cache-once]]. And the phase-sensitivity finding is the quality risk that comes with chunked compression: in models using chunked KV-cache compression, long-context retrieval accuracy can differ by up to 40 percentage points across compression phases at 128K context, with the same information easy at one phase and difficult at another [[periodic-weak-spots]]. At a 4-token stride and 32k context the effect is smaller, but it is present, and a 9B-token run cannot afford to discover it. The recommendation that follows is not "drop CSA2" — it is "pretrain at 8k, adapt to 32k, and measure across phases", which is also cheaper on the fabric because shorter sequences mean smaller activations at the same collective size [[periodic-weak-spots]].

## 7. Verdict: Which Experiments Are Worth Running

The decision set survives. The premises do not. Both statements are true simultaneously, and holding them together is the whole point of this draft.

| Repo claim | Repo's figure | Best available evidence | Tier | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fabric is "PCIe Gen2 x4" | one fact | 0.85 / 1.68 / 2.88 / 6.65 GB/s across four link states | T2 | Under-specified: two independent axes |
| All-reduce 0.75 GiB/s | measured | no receipt; 45–48% of Gen2 x4 link rate; NCCL drives 53% of link at x16 | T1 (user-reported) | Plausible, unfalsifiable, likely pessimistic |
| ~810 TFLOPS bf16 aggregate | 162/card | BF16 164.4–192.7 measured, 202.1 ceiling; 74.6–78.5 at 180 W | T2 | Conservative, inside the band |
| DDP is the only viable topology | yes | TP ~28×; EP ~6× but zero libraries; PP fits 25.8 GB/card | T4 / T3 | Right answer, wrong reason |
| DDP overhead 36% at 1M tokens | 36% | 6.6% under corrected window; 54–58% if busbw-only | T4 | Wrong by 5×, in the safe direction |
| EP ruled out by fabric | ~70× | ~6× arithmetic; no SM80 training EP library exists | T4 / T3 | Right conclusion, wrong arithmetic |
| Frozen tables fit 7B/1B on 64 GB | yes | 44 GB/card if tables total 12 GB; 72 GB if 12 GB/layer | T4 | True, but the repo's own figure breaks it |
| CSA2 pays off at 32k | yes | NSA ≈1.71× full attention end-to-end; sign depends on GQA g | T3 | Feasible; payoff ~half; sign unmeasured |
| 80–120k tok/s at 32k | yes | theoretical max 39.6k at 100% MFU; realistic 9–22k | T4 | Wrong by 2–3× |
| 9B tokens is a sufficient budget | yes | published ablations at 50–350B; CBS reaches 4.2M only at 168B | T3 | Underpowered for architecture claims |

The verdict on each of the repo's four headline claims, in the order the query poses them:

**(a) DDP is the only viable topology at 1M-token global batches.** Correct as a conclusion, wrong as stated. The correct statement is "data-parallel is the only viable *family*", and within it ZeRO-2 is the memory fix that costs nothing in communication and PP=5 is the fallback that costs nothing in memory. Plain DDP cannot hold a 7B model on 64 GB cards; it can hold a 1B-trainable model comfortably.

**(b) EP and TP are ruled out by the fabric.** Correct, but the mechanism is availability rather than cost. TP is genuinely bandwidth-bound at ~28× and can be dismissed on arithmetic. EP is ~6×, not ~70×, and is ruled out because no training-grade EP implementation exists for a no-P2P, no-NVLink, no-RDMA fabric — a software fact, not a physical one. The inference literature shows the fabric is surmountable by specialised kernel work at PCIe 5.0 x16; it does not show it is surmountable at Gen2 x4, and it does not show it is surmountable for training.

**(c) Frozen Engram tables make a 7B-total/~1B-trainable model fit DDP on 64 GB cards.** Correct, and for a better reason than the repo gives. Freezing removes the all-to-all that Engram's own training recipe requires, which is the one collective this fabric cannot run. The design fits at ~44 GB/card if the tables total 12 GB and at ~32 GB/card plus 12.5% PCIe prefetch overhead if they are host-resident. It does not fit if the tables are 12 GB per layer, which is what the repo says. The repo should correct its own table arithmetic and adopt the collective-elimination argument, which is much stronger than the memory argument it currently makes.

**(d) CSA2 compressed sparse attention pays off at 32k training context.** Half right. It is feasible for the substitution arm (FSA and FLA's NSA training run on Ampere) but not for CSA2 specifically without writing an SM80 backward kernel. Its payoff is ~1.71× end-to-end at best and 1.02–1.04× at long context once indexer overhead is counted, and its sign at 32k depends on a GQA group size nobody has measured. Its real value on this box is the KV-cache footprint — 890 bytes per token versus ~35 KB — which is what makes a 1M-token batch fit at all.

What to run, in order of value per GPU-hour:

1. **A five-rank all-reduce receipt.** 256 MiB, bf16, `nccl-tests` `all_reduce_perf`, reporting both algbw and busbw, repeated under all four link states, with a PWRBRK# check before and after. This is the single highest-value measurement in the program, it costs an afternoon, and it settles the numerator of the DDP model, the algbw/busbw question, and the width-versus-generation question at once. Nothing else in this draft is as cheap or as load-bearing.
2. **A sustained BF16 training-shaped benchmark at 250 W.** A short real training loop on the target architecture, not a GEMM peak and not `gpu-burn`. This settles the denominator, which is the widest-confidence item in the entire assessment.
3. **A 500M dense baseline at 8k with batch-size warmup**, not at 32k from step one. This is the control the repo needs and the one the literature supports: 8k pretraining followed by 32k adaptation is the regime every published sparse-attention result was measured in, and it is cheaper on this fabric.
4. **A frozen-table 1B-active model under ZeRO-2**, measuring both the collective-elimination benefit and the host-prefetch overhead. This is the experiment that would validate the repo's most distinctive architectural claim, and it is the one nobody has run.
5. **A scaling-law ladder of 8–12 small runs** rather than one 9B-token run. The scaling-law literature is explicit that many small partially-trained models beat one large one for estimation accuracy, and this box is built for that shape of experiment.

What not to run: expert parallelism, tensor parallelism, from-scratch 32k training, 1M-token batches from step one, and a single 9B-token run presented as evidence for architecture claims. Each of these is either unavailable, uneconomic, or underpowered — and in three of the five cases the reason is not the fabric.

One design principle from the low-compute literature deserves to be carried into the program rather than discovered halfway through it. Per-token training efficiency depends strongly on model size but not on transformer type, and smaller models make up for slower learning efficiency with higher throughput, so all architecture choices converge to roughly the same loss at a fixed time budget — which means architecture selection can be made primarily on how the choice affects computation time for a single gradient step [[cramming-training-a-language-model-on-a]]. On a box where computation time is the binding resource, that is a licence to pick architectures for speed rather than for elegance. It is also a warning: a 9B-token run at 500M parameters will not distinguish architectures on loss, because the literature says it cannot.

The one-sentence inversion, for a reader who will act on it: **the fabric decides what this box can attempt, not what it costs to attempt it — so design for availability, measure for cost, and stop treating 0.75 GiB/s as the number that governs the program.**

## Source Tensions

Six disputes in the source base bear directly on the argument above. Each is stated with both sides, and each is resolved in a way that changes a number rather than a conclusion.

**Width versus generation.** One side holds that PCIe link width and generation are independent axes that must never be conflated: the capacitor mod changes width only and never generation, the unlocker changes generation only and works at any width, and a card with neither runs Gen1 x4. The other side holds that the box is "PCIe Gen2 x4" as a single fact, with every bandwidth figure derived from it. The resolution favours the first side but not in the way it first appears: the repo's premise is under-specified rather than wrong, and the under-specification is the largest single source of uncertainty in the communication model. The four link states span 0.85 to 6.65 GB/s, and the repo's 0.75 GiB/s could be any of them. The load-bearing consequence is that the repo's number sits at less than half the Gen2 x4 one-way rate but within 5% of the Gen1 x4 rate, which means the measurement is either at Gen1 x4 or is a busbw figure — and both readings make the fabric look worse than it is.

**The club's evidence standard versus the repo's.** One side holds that a hardware number is not decision-grade until its full sanitized receipt chain is merged, and that unmerged rows are "pending evidence repair". The other side treats user-reported figures — 0.75 GiB/s all-reduce, 162 TFLOPS bf16, ~70× EP cost — as measured facts carrying the quantitative spine of the program. The resolution is that by the community's own published rule, all three of the repo's load-bearing numbers are pending evidence repair. This is not rhetorical: it is the corpus's own standard applied consistently, and it is why every figure in this draft carries a tier label. But the standard cuts both ways. Demoting the repo's numbers to user-reported does not make them wrong; it makes them unfalsifiable, and in the one case where a direction can be inferred — the all-reduce rate — the error runs against the repo's pessimism rather than its optimism.

**Three incompatible units for effect size.** One side holds that the memory-axis deltas are large: Memory Grafting moves the 0.92B average from 45.62 and 45.03 to 46.98, and the 2.8B average from 51.95/52.43 to 53.86. The other side holds that the deltas are small: published architecture effects are 0.014–0.082 nats against a 3.5%-relative single-run noise floor, so a 9B-token null is the expected result. The resolution is that the two sides are not commensurable. Score points on a ten-task average are not nats of training loss, and neither maps cleanly onto a relative-percentage noise floor. The sharper finding is not "the deltas are below noise" but "**no published Engram or CSA2 delta is reported in a unit in which a noise floor has been established**" — which is falsifiable by a single experiment that reports the delta in nats with two seeds. That experiment is worth more than the 9B-token run it would replace.

**Adaptation-stage results versus from-scratch training.** One side holds that sparse attention delivers large measured 32k wins: NSA reports 6.3× forward and 3.4× backward speedup at 32k on A100 with a quality win, and LongCat's LSA claims parity with full attention and native training to 1M tokens. The other side holds that the repo plans from-scratch 32k training as the primary experiment axis, with 9B tokens at 32k and no adaptation stage. The resolution is that the repo's plan sits outside the regime every published sparse-attention result was measured in. NSA spent 270B tokens at 8k before touching 32k; LongCat's 0.83×-at-32K figure comes from an appendix training-efficiency table, and its abstract's "native training to 1M" is a capability claim, not a from-scratch 32k efficiency claim. The 6.3×/3.4× NSA speedup does not transfer as a from-scratch training number. The recommendation is 8k pretraining followed by 32k adaptation — and, separately, that the from-scratch 64K result at frontier scale (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, 45T tokens, no dense warmup) is the only receipt that supports from-scratch sparse training at all, and it is a receipt for a capability, not for a speedup.

**Two incompatible P2P data-movement figures.** One side holds that BAR1 P2P works and moves data at 1.68 GB/s each way at Gen2 x4 on 12/12 pairs. The other side holds that P2P is absent by default, that the one patch that could enable it half-works (data movement 6.25 GB/s, synchronisation broken, which would leave NCCL hanging), and that even if it worked the link would still be the bottleneck. The resolution is that all three figures are consistent once the link state is separated: 1.68 GB/s is the one-way Gen2 x4 link rate, so P2P at x4 removes a traversal but cannot exceed the link and is not a bandwidth fix; 6.25 GB/s is at x16 and unverified; and the no-P2P baseline at Gen2 x16 already measures 6.21–6.30 GB/s host-staged copy, which saturates the link, so P2P's only real gain there is latency. This resolves in favour of the second side — P2P is a latency lever at x4, not a bandwidth lever — and it means the repo's "no P2P" is not the constraint it thinks it is. The four-card measurement showing P2P *worse* than SHM at N=3 (3.86 vs 4.24 GB/s) and a 25–44% decode regression on a real workload makes the point empirically, not just arithmetically.

**180 W versus 250 W.** One side holds that the decision-grade benchmarks run against a 180.00 W enforced limit, and that the 74.6–78.5 TFLOPS/card figure is a ten-minute `gpu-burn` at that cap with no thermal throttle. The other side holds that the card's stock cap is 250 W and it delivers ~162 TFLOPS bf16, which is the basis of the ~810 TFLOPS aggregate. The resolution is that 74.6–78.5 is a **floor**, not a ceiling: it is measured at 180 W enforced, so at 250 W the sustained number should be higher — but nobody has measured it, and the 162 and 164–192.7 figures are GEMM peaks or FP32 burn-in, not sustained BF16 training at 250 W. The honest training denominator is a range, **75–100 TFLOPS/card**, with the upper end unmeasured. The only figure with a full receipt chain is the 180 W one, and the only figure with a training-shaped workload is the repo's own 91–100 TFLOPS/card end-to-end rate, so those two bound the range from below while the GEMM peaks bound it from above. This is the widest-confidence item in the entire assessment and the second-cheapest one to close [[performancemd]].
