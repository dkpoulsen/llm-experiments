- [[agoq-activation-and-gradient-quantization-for]] — AGoQ: Activation and Gradient Quantization for — AGoQ (Lin et al., HIT Shenzhen + Huawei; arXiv 2605.00539, May 2026) combines two techniques for memory-efficient distributed LLM training: a layer-aware activation quantisation that assigns bit-widths per layer type and pipeline stage to reach near-4-bit activation storage (RMSNorm 4U -> 0.5U, SiLU & Multiply 12U -> 2U, attention stays at 5U; overall activation memory drops from 28U in Megatron-LM to 7.75U, an approximately three-fold reduction), and QuanGrad, an 8-bit (FP8) gradient representation used both for local accumulation and for a "precision-preserved FP8 All-Reduce" implemented as quantise -> reduce-scatter -> re-quantise -> all-gather.

Reported results: up to 52% memory reduction and up to 1.34x training-speed improvement versus Megatron-LM (with and without ZeRO), DeepSpeed and COAT, on 8B-32B LLaMA models, with convergence loss on pretraining and comparable downstream accuracy. Gradient memory drops 75% versus COAT; activation memory drops a further 30% versus COAT.

The most transferable result for this box is their commodity-bandwidth experiment: they explicitly re-ran the collective breakdown at 10 Gbps "to simulate commodity constraints" alongside their 200 Gbps primary testbed. At a 32 MB message under 10 Gbps the decomposed 8-bit all-reduce takes 432.36 ms versus 1,603.78 ms for the baseline - a 3.7x speedup (vs 3.4x at 200 Gbps, 39.17 ms vs 131.23 ms). That is the closest published analogue to the CMP box's ~0.75 GiB/s (~6 Gbps) NCCL fabric: the lower the bandwidth, the LARGER the relative win from halving the payload, which is the opposite of the usual "compression overhead dominates on slow links" intuition.

Caveats: the primary testbed is 64x RTX A6000 over 200 Gb/s InfiniBand (PCIe Gen4-class per-GPU links), and the FP8 path is exercised on NVIDIA Pro 6000 GPUs "to support the FP8 format required by COAT" - again flagging that FP8 needs sm_89+/Hopper-class hardware. GA100 (sm_80) has no native FP8, so on the CMP 170HX the 8-bit path would need int8 or emulated FP8, and the paper does not measure that. `review`

- [[communication-efficient-distributed-training]] — Communication-Efficient Distributed Training — Munim & Ramamoorthy (arXiv 2609.23898v2, cs.IT, 26 Sep 2026) propose CERAR, a Communication-Efficient Ring All-Reduce protocol that trades exactness for bandwidth: each worker splits its gradient into c chunks, linearly encodes them (Vandermonde, or a condition-number-constrained construction), and runs L = c + N - 2 rounds over an N-worker ring, giving normalized per-worker communication 1 + (N-2)/c instead of ring all-reduce's 2(N-1)/N, at the cost of a bounded approximation error (~1.6-2.9% relative l2 gradient error in their training runs). The Vandermonde route is asymptotically exact as c grows but ill-conditioned; they give an alternate scheme with error O(eps) and condition number O(eps^-r*), r* = ceil(c/N) - 1, plus a constrained optimization for practical designs.

The headline empirical result lands on the fabric that matters for this box: on a single node of 8x A100 PCIe (interconnect characterised as 31.5 GB/s), CERAR cuts raw all-reduce time by 46.0-47.1% versus NCCL - a 1B-entry FP32 tensor goes from 17,954.4 ms (NCCL) to 9,558.6 ms (CERAR); 500M entries 8,611.2 -> 4,651.0 ms; 100M 1,775.9 -> 940.1 ms. On 4x H200 SXM over NVLink (>=300 GB/s) the same scheme is 33.2-33.9% SLOWER than NCCL (24B entries: 392.049 ms NCCL vs 522.366 ms CERAR), so the win is explicitly a low-bandwidth-fabric phenomenon, not a general one.

End-to-end: Pythia-1.4B on 4x A100 PCIe 40GB for 1,800 steps (~117.96M tokens) takes 3.79 h with NCCL vs 3.14 h with CERAR (-17.3%, ~39 min saved), throughput 8,644 -> 10,451 tok/s, final validation loss 1.4218 vs 1.4213 (essentially identical), peak memory 36.2 vs 38.6 GB. On 4x A100 SXM 80GB over NVLink CERAR is 3.65% slower (4.745 h vs 4.578 h).

Caveats the drafter must carry: (i) the authors call their implementation a proof of concept, not optimised to NCCL's level (NCCL uses multiple channels and compute/comm overlap); (ii) encoding/decoding compute is real and grows with c, so on a box whose GPUs are also weak the arithmetic overhead is not free; (iii) the tested PCIe fabric is 31.5 GB/s, roughly 30x the ~0.75 GiB/s NCCL all-reduce measured on the CMP 170HX box, so the communication-bound regime is far deeper here - the direction of the effect should hold, the magnitude is not transferable; (iv) the approximation error is ~2-3% relative on gradients, a training-quality risk the paper argues is benign at 1.4B scale only. `review`
- [[geofp8-geometry-aware-fp8-gradient]] — GeoFP8: Geometry-Aware FP8 Gradient — GeoFP8 (Wang et al., Rutgers/NCSU; arXiv 2607.07494) attacks low-precision gradient communication from the geometry side rather than the format side. Diagnosis: gradients are highly anisotropic, so axis-aligned (Euclidean) FP8 scaling incurs direction-dependent distortion; transforming gradients into a near-isotropic space before quantisation - using a block-diagonal K-FAC local metric, applied selectively and approximated with a rank-32 input-side low-rank factor - makes the FP8 representation substantially more faithful. GeoFP8 deliberately does not change the optimizer, training recipe, collective, or low-precision format; only the coordinate system used for the low-precision communication.

Numbers: GeoFP8 reduces the error between FP8-averaged and FP32-averaged gradients by up to 67.4% over the Euclidean baseline; it cuts gradient communication volume by 75.0% relative to FP32, yielding a 7.6% end-to-end pretraining-time reduction for Llama-600M on 64 NVIDIA GH200 Superchips. Direct Euclidean FP8 at the same scale gives a 10.79% end-to-end reduction, i.e. GeoFP8 deliberately trades ~3 points of speed for downstream-task preservation. Experiments use 32 GPUs, global batch 512, micro-batch 4, Muon optimizer, lr 5e-4, on TACC Vista GH200 nodes (96 GB HBM3 per GPU, NVLink-coupled Grace-Hopper).

Two caveats matter for the CMP 170HX box. (1) The 7.6% end-to-end figure is measured on a fast NVLink fabric where gradient communication is a minority of step time; on a ~1 GB/s PCIe fabric the same 75% payload cut would translate into a much larger fraction of step time, so the paper's end-to-end number understates the potential gain here. (2) FP8 is not natively supported on GA100/sm_80 - the CMP 170HX is Ampere-generation silicon without FP8 tensor-core paths, so an FP8 gradient path would need software emulation whose cost is not accounted for in the GH200 measurements. Their BF16-vs-FP32 comparison is also relevant to the repo's bf16 gradient plan: BF16 gradient communication "matches FP32 on 7 out of 14 downstream tasks" but with slightly lower absolute values, which is why they kept FP32 as the reference. `review`

- [[zipccl-efficient-lossless-data-compression-of]] — ZipCCL: Efficient Lossless Data Compression of — ZipCCL (Lin, Pan, Fan, Shi, Chu; arXiv 2604.27844) is a lossless compression layer for NCCL collectives in LLM training. Core observation: LLM training tensors (activations, gradients, parameters) are near-Gaussian, so BF16 exponents are highly concentrated - the top-7 most frequent exponents cover >97% of the data. ZipCCL encodes each BF16 exponent in 3 bits (7 codes plus a zero-point escape flag) instead of 8, leaving sign/mantissa untouched, for a theoretical compression ratio up to 11 bits from 16 (~1.45x). It adds GPU-optimised compress/decompress kernels with communication-aware data layout and an adaptive switcher driven by a cost model over measured bandwidth, latency and compression ratio.

Evaluation: 64-GPU cluster of 8x RTX A6000 per node over a 200 Gb/s network, integrated into Megatron-LM (MoE all-to-all) and TorchTitan (FSDP all-gather/reduce-scatter), plus a 16xH800 400 Gb/s cluster. Communication speedups over NCCL: 1.25x on DeepSeek-V3 all-to-all, 1.24x on Qwen3-MoE, 1.35x on Llama3-8B all-gather; averaged across message sizes 1.36x for all-to-all and 1.31x for all-gather. End-to-end training speedups: 1.16x (DeepSeek-V3), 1.16x (Qwen3-MoE), 1.18x (Llama3-8B); on the 16xH800 cluster 1.13x for both Qwen3-MoE and Llama3-8B.

Two findings are load-bearing for this box. First, the benefit is bandwidth-saturation dependent: "When the data volume is small, bandwidth cannot be fully utilized, and our ZipCCL shows very close performance to NCCL. However, as the data volume increases ... and bandwidth is better saturated, the improvement ... becomes significant." On a ~1 GB/s fabric the saturation point arrives at far smaller tensors, so 1.3x is a floor rather than a ceiling. Second, Reduce-Scatter was NOT worth compressing on their platform ("the original Reduce-Scatter implementation was found to be faster"), so a DDP/FSDP gradient path does not automatically inherit the gain - all-gather did, reduce-scatter did not.

Also notable: ZipCCL beats DietGPU (Meta's GPU lossless compressor), where DietGPU-compressed all-gather was slower than plain NCCL all-gather while ZipCCL got 1.35x. The scheme is bit-exact, so unlike FP8/quantisation work it carries no convergence risk; the relevant risk on this box is compress/decompress kernel cost against 5 weak GPUs. `review`
