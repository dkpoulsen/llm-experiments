# Tag: quantization

**3** notes.

- [[agoq-activation-and-gradient-quantization-for]] — AGoQ: Activation and Gradient Quantization for — AGoQ (Lin et al., HIT Shenzhen + Huawei; arXiv 2605.00539, May 2026) combines two techniques for memory-efficient distributed LLM training: a layer-aware activation quantisation that assigns bit-widths per layer type and pipeline stage to reach near-4-bit activation storage (RMSNorm 4U -> 0.5U, SiLU & Multiply 12U -> 2U, attention stays at 5U; overall activation memory drops from 28U in Megatron-LM to 7.75U, an approximately three-fold reduction), and QuanGrad, an 8-bit (FP8) gradient representation used both for local accumulation and for a "precision-preserved FP8 All-Reduce" implemented as quantise -> reduce-scatter -> re-quantise -> all-gather.

Reported results: up to 52% memory reduction and up to 1.34x training-speed improvement versus Megatron-LM (with and without ZeRO), DeepSpeed and COAT, on 8B-32B LLaMA models, with convergence loss on pretraining and comparable downstream accuracy. Gradient memory drops 75% versus COAT; activation memory drops a further 30% versus COAT.

The most transferable result for this box is their commodity-bandwidth experiment: they explicitly re-ran the collective breakdown at 10 Gbps "to simulate commodity constraints" alongside their 200 Gbps primary testbed. At a 32 MB message under 10 Gbps the decomposed 8-bit all-reduce takes 432.36 ms versus 1,603.78 ms for the baseline - a 3.7x speedup (vs 3.4x at 200 Gbps, 39.17 ms vs 131.23 ms). That is the closest published analogue to the CMP box's ~0.75 GiB/s (~6 Gbps) NCCL fabric: the lower the bandwidth, the LARGER the relative win from halving the payload, which is the opposite of the usual "compression overhead dominates on slow links" intuition.

Caveats: the primary testbed is 64x RTX A6000 over 200 Gb/s InfiniBand (PCIe Gen4-class per-GPU links), and the FP8 path is exercised on NVIDIA Pro 6000 GPUs "to support the FP8 format required by COAT" - again flagging that FP8 needs sm_89+/Hopper-class hardware. GA100 (sm_80) has no native FP8, so on the CMP 170HX the 8-bit path would need int8 or emulated FP8, and the paper does not measure that. `review`
- [[geofp8-geometry-aware-fp8-gradient]] — GeoFP8: Geometry-Aware FP8 Gradient — GeoFP8 (Wang et al., Rutgers/NCSU; arXiv 2607.07494) attacks low-precision gradient communication from the geometry side rather than the format side. Diagnosis: gradients are highly anisotropic, so axis-aligned (Euclidean) FP8 scaling incurs direction-dependent distortion; transforming gradients into a near-isotropic space before quantisation - using a block-diagonal K-FAC local metric, applied selectively and approximated with a rank-32 input-side low-rank factor - makes the FP8 representation substantially more faithful. GeoFP8 deliberately does not change the optimizer, training recipe, collective, or low-precision format; only the coordinate system used for the low-precision communication.

Numbers: GeoFP8 reduces the error between FP8-averaged and FP32-averaged gradients by up to 67.4% over the Euclidean baseline; it cuts gradient communication volume by 75.0% relative to FP32, yielding a 7.6% end-to-end pretraining-time reduction for Llama-600M on 64 NVIDIA GH200 Superchips. Direct Euclidean FP8 at the same scale gives a 10.79% end-to-end reduction, i.e. GeoFP8 deliberately trades ~3 points of speed for downstream-task preservation. Experiments use 32 GPUs, global batch 512, micro-batch 4, Muon optimizer, lr 5e-4, on TACC Vista GH200 nodes (96 GB HBM3 per GPU, NVLink-coupled Grace-Hopper).

Two caveats matter for the CMP 170HX box. (1) The 7.6% end-to-end figure is measured on a fast NVLink fabric where gradient communication is a minority of step time; on a ~1 GB/s PCIe fabric the same 75% payload cut would translate into a much larger fraction of step time, so the paper's end-to-end number understates the potential gain here. (2) FP8 is not natively supported on GA100/sm_80 - the CMP 170HX is Ampere-generation silicon without FP8 tensor-core paths, so an FP8 gradient path would need software emulation whose cost is not accounted for in the GH200 measurements. Their BF16-vs-FP32 comparison is also relevant to the repo's bf16 gradient plan: BF16 gradient communication "matches FP32 on 7 out of 14 downstream tasks" but with slightly lower absolute values, which is why they kept FP32 as the reference. `review`
- [[zero-extremely-efficient-collective-communication-for-giant]] — ZeRO++: Extremely Efficient Collective Communication for Giant — ZeRO++ (Microsoft): three communication-volume reductions aimed at exactly the two regimes this box sits in - low-bandwidth clusters and small per-GPU batches. It restates ZeRO's baseline cost as 3M total volume spread over 2 all-gathers and 1 reduce-scatter, then (1) qwZ quantizes weights FP16 to INT8 for the forward all-gather, halving that collective's volume, with block-wise quantization to protect accuracy; (2) hpZ keeps a full model copy inside each machine and replaces the cross-machine backward all-gather with an intra-machine all-gather, trading memory for bandwidth; (3) qgZ replaces the reduce-scatter with an all-to-all-based quantized gradient averaging that preserves accuracy despite low-precision data. Net: 4x lower communication volume than ZeRO, up to 2.16x throughput at 384 GPUs (up to 2.4x over ZeRO in the body). Notes that per-GPU throughput on low-bandwidth clusters is only half that of high-bandwidth clusters, and that cross-node links are often under 100 Gbps Ethernet. `review`
