- [[cramming-training-a-language-model-on-a]] — CRAMMING: TRAINING A LANGUAGE MODEL ON A — Cramming (Geiping & Goldstein, arXiv 2212.14034) is the canonical single-GPU compute-budget study: train a transformer LM from scratch with masked LM on one GPU for 24 hours and measure GLUE. Hardware tested: RTX 2080 Ti (11 GB), RTX A4000 (16 GB), RTX A6000 (48 GB), each paired with 4 CPU cores and 32 GB RAM; the A6000 run reaches BERT-base-class GLUE in one day.

Findings that bear on CMP 170HX feasibility: (1) scaling laws hold in the extreme low-compute regime — per-token training efficiency depends strongly on model size but not on transformer type, so architecture changes only help insofar as they raise throughput at constant parameter count; (2) the authors explicitly find no benefit from CPU/NVMe offloading in this setting; (3) FlashAttention (Dao et al. 2022) is only re-enabled for the final architecture variant after ablations, i.e. it is a throughput lever, not an accuracy lever; (4) the ablation accounting attributes ~2 GLUE points to architecture changes, ~1 point to data changes, and ~0.5 point to training-recipe changes; (5) sequence length 128 with packed sequences gives micro-batches of 64-96 on the 2080 Ti, accumulated into larger batches, and single-epoch training is feasible at this budget.

Relevance: it is the strongest published evidence that a single GPU with no interconnect at all can produce a usable model in a bounded wall-clock budget — the limiting case of the CMP 170HX box (5 cards, no NVLink, no P2P). It also warns that offloading, a plausible workaround for a 64 GB-per-card memory ceiling, bought nothing in their measurements. `review`

- [[lisa-linear-indexed-sparse-attention-for-efficient]] — LISA: Linear-Indexed Sparse Attention for Efficient — LISA (Zhao et al., Alibaba International Digital Commerce + Dalian University of Technology + Amazon, arXiv 2607.19358) is a plug-and-play attention replacement that requires NO pretraining from scratch: a Linear Attention branch (O(n) time, long-range memory) runs in parallel with a Lightning Indexer that selects the top-M tokens for a Sparse Self-Attention, the two branches fused by a gating mechanism, reducing inference complexity from O(n^2) to O(nM) for generating n tokens.

Two-stage training: Stage 1 cold-starts the linear attention plus a sliding-window attention mechanism, optimized by knowledge distillation to approximate the full self-attention distribution of a frozen teacher; Stage 2 introduces the Indexer to replace the static sliding window, trained with a per-head KL divergence loss aligning its selection behaviour with the teacher's attention patterns. All original model parameters are frozen — only the newly added linear-attention and Indexer modules train. Because top-M is argmax, gradients cannot flow back through the selection; early in training they select top-1.2M candidates and randomly mask out 20%, gradually reducing the dropout ratio, and apply z-loss to the Indexer.

Budget analysis: MASS (attention mass captured) reaches 80% at M=256, only 6% below the theoretical oracle upper bound; 70% at M=128 and 88% at M=512, so M=256 is the recommended balance. Results on DeepSeek-distilled Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B: 50% inference speedup at 16K context with +5.6% average reasoning accuracy; on Qwen2.5-7B, 70.6% average accuracy vs 263.3 -> 226.6 relative latency measured on a single A100 (batch 1, BF16, eager mode). Training cost: each of the two stages takes approximately 240 GPU-hours on an NVIDIA H100 (~480 GPU-hours total) for the 7B retrofit, with Stage 1 at LR 2e-5, batch 128, 2 epochs, 50-step warmup. 10,747 words. `review`
- [[longcat-sparse-attention-taming-the-lightning-via]] — LongCat Sparse Attention: Taming the Lightning via — LongCat Sparse Attention (LSA; Zan et al., Meituan LongCat Team, arXiv 2608.01662) is a hardware-algorithm co-design of the DSA lineage with three orthogonal components: Streaming-Aware Indexing (SI — converts scattered KV entries into hardware-aligned contiguous layouts for coalesced HBM access), Cross-Layer Indexing (CLI — one layer's indexer result reused across consecutive layers, supported by cross-layer distillation), and Hierarchical Indexing (HI — coarse-to-fine candidate narrowing, training-free and plug-and-play). Scaling experiments span 69B-A3B to 560B-A27B models; LSA supports native training to 1M context and underpins LongCat-2.0 (1.6T-A48B); LongCat-Flash-Lite-Sparse (69B-A3B) is open-sourced.

Measured facts that bear directly on the training-cost question: (a) the sparse-attention operator is severely memory-bound — about 4.5% of peak HBM bandwidth (~1/22) because of discontinuous gather patterns (~6% memory-level parallelism, ~75% cacheline packing efficiency); (b) the backward pass is worse than forward because gradient accumulation allocates a full-sequence-length HBM buffer and executes scatter_add over the selected sparse index set, and overlapping indices across cores serialize writes to shared cachelines; (c) the indexer accounts for up to 90% of per-layer latency at 1024K context, and indexer latency grows 27x from 0.034 ms to 0.930 ms while sparse-attention latency stays ~0.10 ms; (d) at 32K sequence length LSA training is a NET LOSS versus dense MLA — 1.08x forward, 0.79x backward, 0.83x total — with crossover at 64K (1.39x), 2.21x at 128K, 3.39x at 256K, 7.73x at 1024K, and the practical crossover moves to 128K once variable-length packing is accounted for; (e) core-attention training latency at qlen=8192, K=2048, BF16: SFA fwd 23.64 ms / bwd 238.60 ms at 32K KV (backward ~10x forward), HFA 19.70 / 137.77 ms; HFA accelerates core attention up to 1.91x forward and 1.73x backward.

Index-quality and training-recipe facts: adjacent layers share 57.4% of their Top-K budget and reusing an adjacent layer's index set still captures 93.2% of the target layer's attention mass; K=2048 at 128K context gives ~98.4% sparsity with near-lossless quality; sink+SWA regions capture ~83% of attention mass on average across layers. Training is two-stage: a dense warm-up where the indexer is supervised against the full-sequence attention distribution, then sparse training where the distillation target is the attention distribution renormalized over the selected subset; the fixed-to-sparse budget ratio is ~1:1 (Kswa=1024, Ksparse=1024, Ksink=16), and during training the sink/SWA tokens are deliberately included in the distillation target because they carry substantial attention mass. Cross-layer index reuse without training adaptation degrades quality, which is why CLI requires cross-layer distillation in both the dense warm-up and sparse stages. HI is training-free and only enabled beyond a crossover point (net slowdown of 0.79-0.82x below it). 30,152 words — long source, flag for source-analyst. `review`
- [[over-tokenized-transformer-vocabulary-is-generally-worth-scaling]] — Over-Tokenized Transformer: Vocabulary is Generally Worth Scaling — OverEncoding / Over-Tokenized Transformer (ByteDance Seed, arXiv 2501.16975, ICML 2025) - the direct prior-art baseline Engram compares against. Decouples input and output vocabularies and scales the input vocabulary with hierarchical n-gram tokens; finds a log-linear relationship between input vocabulary size and training loss, so OE-12.8M matches a 2.5x larger baseline at no extra training cost (400M OE vs 1B baseline), with a 5.7x convergence speedup on OLMo2-1B at 1T tokens and 3.2x/3.0x/2.6x downstream accelerations. MOST IMPORTANT PART FOR THIS QUERY is Section 3.3 'Engineering Challenges and Solutions', which is the only source in this batch that reports measured training-time cost of large embedding tables under real sharding strategies: (a) with plain FSDP, the sparse embedding parameters' communication severely degrades training efficiency and FSDP 'easily runs out of memory for vocabulary sizes exceeding m = 5x10^6'; (b) without mitigation FSDP experiences a 25% slowdown; (c) the recommended fix is tensor parallelism specifically for the embedding layer - row-wise sharding across all DP ranks with the token sent to the owning rank and the vector sent back, i.e. two all-to-all in the forward pass and one all-to-all in the backward pass, 'a total communication volume significantly lower than that of FSDP'; (d) with that optimization, training an over-encoded model at m = 10^7 on FSDP loses less than 5% throughput. The paper also proposes, but does not measure, a dedicated embedding-lookup pipeline stage that would overlap embedding communication with the transformer forward of the current micro-batch and allow over-encoding parameters to be offloaded to CPU, citing Fang et al. 2022 and Li et al. 2023. MoE results: OLMoE-1.3B +OE-12.8M goes 1.3B -> 13.1B params with loss -0.082 and downstream +0.014; OLMoE-7B +OE-12.8M goes 7B -> 26.3B with loss -0.076 and downstream +0.007, at 500B tokens. `review`
- [[smollm2-when-smol-goes-big]] — SmolLM2: When Smol Goes Big — — SmolLM2 (Hugging Face, arXiv 2502.02737) is the reference recipe for training a ~1.7B model from scratch on a fixed compute budget, and the primary source for the FineMath / Stack-Edu / SmolTalk datasets. Base model: 1.7B params, Llama-2 architecture, 49,152-token tokenizer, ~11T tokens over four stages on 256 H100s with the nanotron framework; total cost stated as ~1e23 FLOPs, i.e. ~$250,000 of GPU compute — cited as the reason the authors rebalance mixtures online instead of running multiple from-scratch runs. Stage 1 (0-6T tokens) is 60% FineWeb-Edu / 40% DCLM plus 10% StarCoder-Data; later stages add OWM/InfiMM-WebMath/FineMath and Stack-Edu at 14-24% code and 4-10% math. Ablation models are 1.7B params, sequence length 2048, global batch ~2M tokens, LR 3e-4 cosine, 350B tokens per ablation.

FineMath: up to 54B tokens of math from Common Crawl, built by re-extracting 7.7B URLs (5.7B classifier-identified + 0.6B OWM + 1.3B InfiMM-WebMath) with the OWM pipeline to preserve LaTeX, then scoring with a Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-annotated classifier on a 5-point scale. FineMath4+ = 10B tokens / 6.7M docs; FineMath3+ = 34B tokens / 21.4M docs. Reported: FineMath4+ gives a 2x GSM8K and 6x MATH improvement over InfiMM-WebMath; Infi-WebMath4+ plateaus after ~80B tokens (~10 epochs) from repetition, a plateau not seen in FineMath4+.

Stack-Edu: filtered variant of StarCoder2Data restricted to the 15 largest languages (~450B-token subset); 15 language-specific StarEncoder classifiers trained on Llama3-70B-Instruct synthetic annotations (0-5 educational scale, 500k samples each, F1 > 0.7 at threshold 3). Result: ~125B tokens across 15 languages; Python 50.6B -> 21.8B with HumanEval 20.7 -> 25.6, C++ 69.7B -> 16.0B with MultiPL-E 16.7 -> 24.8.

Context extension: 2k -> 8k by continuing from a stage-4 checkpoint with RoPE base 130k and a mixture of 40% long documents (>=8k tokens) from DCLM (10%), FineWeb-Edu (10%) and Dolma books (20%). Relevance to the CMP 170HX run: it establishes that a 1.7B-class model at 2k context with a ~2M-token global batch is a normal, published ablation configuration — the scale this box can plausibly reach — and gives concrete token counts for the three datasets in the repo's data pipeline. `review`

- [[training-compute-optimal-large-language-models]] — Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Hoffmann et al., DeepMind, arXiv 2203.15556) is the primary behind the '20 tokens per parameter' rule that FineWeb and SmolLM2 both use to size their ablations. The paper fits three independent approaches — minimum over training curves, IsoFLOP profiles, and parametric loss modelling — on models from 70M to over 16B parameters trained on 5B to over 400B tokens, and finds that model size and training tokens should be scaled in approximately equal proportions as compute grows: exponents a = 0.50 / b = 0.50 (approach 1), 0.49 / 0.51 (approach 2), 0.46 / 0.54 (approach 3), versus Kaplan et al.'s 0.73 / 0.27.

The practical frontier (Table 3, approach 1) gives exact FLOPs and token counts: 400M params -> 1.92e19 FLOPs / 8.0B tokens; 1B -> 1.21e20 FLOPs / 20.2B tokens; 10B -> 1.23e22 FLOPs / 205.1B tokens; 67B -> 5.76e23 FLOPs / 1.5T tokens; 175B -> 3.85e24 / 3.7T; 1T -> 1.27e26 / 21.2T. The FLOPs constraint used throughout is FLOPs(N,D) = 6ND.

Headline result: Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) trained on the same compute budget as Gopher (280B) uniformly and significantly outperforms Gopher, GPT-3 (175B) and Jurassic-1 (178B), reaching 67.5% on MMLU, a >7% improvement over Gopher. The paper's central claim is that the models of its era were considerably over-sized relative to their compute budgets.

Why it matters here: 6ND plus the Table 3 frontier converts the box's ~810 TFLOPS bf16 into a concrete answer to 'how big a model and how many tokens can this run afford' — a 1B model at 20.2B tokens costs 1.21e20 FLOPs, a multi-day-to-multi-week run at realistic MFU, while a 10B model at 205B tokens (1.23e22 FLOPs) is out of reach. It also justifies the repo's choice of a ~1B-trainable model rather than a larger one at fixed compute. `review`
